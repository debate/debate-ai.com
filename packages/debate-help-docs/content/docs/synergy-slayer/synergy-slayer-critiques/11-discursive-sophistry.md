## **Part III. The Costs**

10. ### **Discursive Sound-Bite Sophistry Masks Ignoring Real Harms**

    1. The harm is to assume one can simply cherry-pick a problematic discursive mindset and win just on that. That has never been and never will be how real-world debate works. One generally needs dozens of claims extended to scrutinize assumptions. Many policies may have an unethical tinge, and that is weighed in relation. We should emulate the "minor amendments" clause under contract law: discursive mistakes that are not central to the substance may not affect enforceability (Eisenberg, 2009). If the negative can win just by advocating rephrasing discourse, then debate will become a best-speech contest, and the negative can win by translating to promote multi-culture. "Study of discourse enables one to stand aside from issues and avoid any commitment to a cause or ideal, simply presenting all sides of a debate and pointing out the discursive strategies involved. As the physical world appears to fade into mere discourse, so it comes to seem less real than the language used to describe it; and environmental issues lose the dimensions of urgency" (Kidner, 2000, pp. 66–67). "Their preoccupation with representation, sign systems, and with the signifier over the signified, leaves one with little sense of the annihilation visited upon the people and land of Iraq. And, as the Vietnam War proved and Schwarzkopf well realized, without that physicalistic sense of violence, war can be more effectively sold to a jingoistic public." (Krishna, 1993, p. 399). Boggs holds that vital issues “cannot be understood outside the larger social and global context,” so their critical authors would want discourse examined alongside policy analysis, such as calls to distrust securitized impacts in tabloid news. It falsely conflates to project rejecting a flawed mindset as affecting broader change, when it forecloses space for how to persuade change. It teaches a bad practice of looking for petty nitpicks to dramatize taking offense to so as to reject debating those mindsets, which only alienates and fails to persuade those common mindsets. A toxic culture of exaggerated taking offense frustrates researchers, tokenizing nitpicks from ethics scholars who would not want such namecalling to defeat our common purpose. Students cajole judges into thinking they are being more ethical in order to tokenize any criticisms they would rather talk about instead. Cheapshots inspire similar tactics, until issue researchers depart for more meaningful communities, leaving the rejectionists with no one to strawperson. These debates teach nothing useless other than to nitpick to manipulate groups to exclude people with controversial views. Looking to take offense to ignoring every other concern a person raises is repugnant fascist thought policing and it is selectively enforced just as an excuse to exclude those with unpopular viewpoints – exactly the speech that the First Amendment was aimed to protect. If one cannot handle free speech of controversial arguments, then they do not belong in debate. Voting against someone does not cause racism to get “rejected” and only means people self-isolate from changing mindsets, thus ceding the political to far-right rhetoric, while leftist debate makes a mockery of itself as it looks for trivial nitpicks to exclude and spread toxic negativity about some, who then retaliate worse ways.

The negative falsely oversells: they have no quote saying any one claim precludes evaluating the case. Their authors are hoping to help shape sectors—yet they twist them to reject learning to persuade on specifics. Debate is still a game, and it skews the game if critiques are twisted to not answer feasible plans. "That is, rather than discuss the actual costs or benefits of Dole's tax plan, citizens instead focused upon the rhetorical viability \[…\] Many of the public policy discussions degenerated into cynical exchanges that steered discussion away from the possibility of solving communal problems" (Carlin & Levasseur, 2001, p. 420). Discourse authors are hoping to improve the field, like suggesting better affirmative speech phrasing, and would not want to be used to reject listening to mainstream views. It is an unfair burden as it is too easy to suggest how discourse can always be better, but it is not a reason to reject what is good enough in times of crisis, judged as what is persuasive. Society is already toxified by those who've been co-opted into spreading hatred while justifying that to themselves as feeling ethically superior in “rejecting” those they deem inferior to justify ignoring the harm to them and counter-productive backlash. Disrupt this echo-chamber commoditizing nitpicks to teach prejudice.
