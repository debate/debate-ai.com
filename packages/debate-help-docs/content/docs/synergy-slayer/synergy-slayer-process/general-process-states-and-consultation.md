# Defending the General Process, States, and Consultation

## We Defend the General Process’s Consequences and Assume Modifications
Galloway (2019) shows the negative wins the consult debate only by redefining **"should" and "resolved" to mean immediate and certain**, borrowing esoteric jury-instruction and mistrial caselaw to force the aff into a live-action-roleplay of instant, unconditional passage that no real reader of the case literature ever demanded. That redefinition is the same illusion Section 1 rejects: fiat is not a video game in which the judge stopwatches the plan's implementation, it is a test of whether an interested reader, evaluating the should-claim in the context the author wrote it, would still back the policy. Once immediacy and certainty are exposed as artifacts of definitional gymnastics rather than plan mandates, the consult CP's delay and its ally veto stop being reasons to reject the plan and become, at best, **extrinsic scheduling questions** — precisely the kind of “party leaders know how to schedule” tradeoff that a concerned public does not project onto its support for the underlying should-statement. The affirmative defends the resolution after the colon introducing "a formal resolution, after the word 'resolved:'" (Enlisted Leader Research Group, 2022). Teams may not test immediacy, certainty, or process by redefining “Resolved” which is not in the resolution itself. The Constitution’s founders already knew what it meant when they wrote the Articles of Confederation. We are debating now to gather support, **which happens immediately at the moment the judge is “resolved**” to vote that a concerned reader would still find an intrinsic benefit to support the policy. We do not act like a plan happens now as modern society lacks the technology for such immediate passage. Fiat does not mean roleplaying wizardry like a live-action video game. Debate’s research comparison comes from analyzing the literature base, not semantics. Even if we delegate to states or consult bindingly with another institution then the case speech would occur at that time: the states would use the affirmative to persuade values at the federal level; also, **NATO would *then* debate** whether the U.S. government should do the plan. Quotes can only be evaluated in their context of opportunity costs to persuade the public, not as simulated scenarios. The counterplan results in forcing the debate, thus proving the case persuasive to a reader. Supporting an advocacy the plan should be done as a principle does not give up the opportunity to solicit prior support elsewhere. One can shift the roleplaying analogy stage from online articles to Congress to NATO to a philosophy classroom; preferred analogy is irrelevant to “fiat” as all support as a principle that society should follow the only plan to survive. The affirmative assumes supporting the plan through the usual process, which is consistent with **supporting in principle also changing that process**. Two lobbyist groups could convince the same institution and would not pick the plan as the specific issue to split on. We can permute their general cause of deferring all other decisions to consult NATO, which forces the same debate and does not prevent a citizen from supporting. Mooting the case to shift to another literature base gives the illusion that it can always be better if done by states, in consultation with NATO, or after the Marxist revolution, etc. But that would moot debate on any issue and be a separate topic. We are not here to educate in general, since we can be experts in fields later. Our unique value is persuasive detailed research which forms a common purpose. We can only dig in-depth and persuasively resolve differences in one literature base topic. Students would be seen as irrelevant in switching topics like a politician dodging questions in the real world. There’s no reason to hold it against this specific plan, as we can shift to consultation in general, showing that this is a contrived hypothetical. It is always possible to delay with infinite ways it could be slightly better. Consult counterplans fail the concerned-reader standard for the identical reason the states counterplan does: they manufacture non-intrinsic tradeoffs and then dress the manufacture up as competition.

## We Are *Internally* Resolved, Regardless of Consultation as a Symbolic Formality
The concerned readers are still convinced by the case to be resolved immediately in support of the plan, but they also follow the suggestion for a better process, strongly believing that NATO will approve. We cannot predict what would happen, only read articles on what *should* be based on values. The fact that the plan might be rejected or fail – has been said about *any* plan can go awry: “The best-laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley” (Burns, 1786). We assume only the literature shown in the round is true: this shows consensus, so any concerned reader would be so strongly resolved in the plan they’d also want to convince NATO to approve it. At worst, the advocates of the plan would be lying to themselves internally while accepting that asking approval is the new way domestic politics is done. Diplomats often have to be two-faced to appease a foreign state and let them think they have authority. Recchia (2016) explains U.S. policymakers often seek approval from bodies such as NATO not primarily because the organization can compel them – or **because they need permission in a *literal* sense** – but to create legitimacy around an intervention as multilateral and to facilitate financial **burden sharing to get approval from Congress**. No readers would find persuasive that policymakers should refuse to do any policy or intervene in a country unless NATO completely agrees to it and **holds power over future** such policies. Debaters have completely misapplied this literature which only says on some foreign wars to seek support from others before action, which is how most policies happen. No public would assume that one of the institutions should have binding control over all other states’ future actions. If NATO rejects burden sharing, U.**S. policymakers still face pressure to act through a revised policy,** a different coalition, or a newly framed contingency which the media will say is another issue. This may seem like “lying” about being resolved, but that assumes a single consistent agent, yet a party builds coalitions across mindsets using two-faced contradictions (Malhotra, 2015). Consultation debaters have misunderstood “fiat” like role-playing a scenario of what would happen, but we can only debate at the level of what **“should” persuade** a concerned reader**.** We can only debate if consultation is discussed in specific detail in topic literature, or we resort to creative charlatanry to fool against sensible policy. No literature authors write about why the U.S. should do the plan but another institution *shouldn’t* also support that it be done. The authors want consultations for military and financial burden sharing in a war zone, not simply asking NATO to decide for the U.S. its policies. The permutation “do the counterplan,”  “consult on other issues,” or “do both” results in the plan, even if NATO says no: proving the net benefit is an advantage to consulting in general, not intrinsic to *this* plan.

## The Reality of Uniform State Action
**Requiring fifty states to enact first-time uniform legislation may trigger federal action**. Compacts for uniformity are “'upgraded' to the status of federal law” necessitating congressional consent, and "interstate compacts receiving congressional consent are now clearly **recognized as equivalent to federal law**" (Shapiro, 2016). States interpret statutes differently due to divergent caselaw, budgets, and political coalitions. Large states can use their market power to “compel nationwide compliance” with their policies, effectively overriding other states and displacing Congress’s role (Manne et al., 2025). Under the Commerce Clause, the federal government has both the authority and “responsibility” to prevent one state’s policies from imposing cross-state harms, making this situation a clear case for federal intervention. As a result, policymakers may need to clarify how state unfair-competition laws interact with federal antitrust standards in markets with “inherently interstate” spillover forcing Congress to act. These differences create pressure for Congressional standardization under the Commerce Clause delegation under *Lopez* (1995), and federal compensation for uneven state funding. If their text says states and territories should do the plan, this advocates federal action on federal military territories such as Wake Island. The negative cannot fiat true uniformity across fifty states, any more than fiating universal kindness. It produces patchworks requiring federal coordination. Either the permutation to do the counterplan preserves federal agency action or doing both results in delegation where federal agencies still operate alongside state implementation. Federal action remains intact while shielding political blame. **Political hypotheticals are the same** when news polarizes the issue via party association. Governors will ride coattails as presidential candidates. No decider can choose between state or federal legislative fiat, thus negatives are limited to what institutions authors call the public to support.

## The Permutation: Inevitable Agency Coordination
Allowing fiating state action is unprincipled and opens the floodgates to the opposing party using this tactic on most plans. The negative is self-contradictory by calling to delegate all cases and plans to the states because Congress has more important issues, because their own advocates would categorically delegate to the states even the federal issues of their political scenarios. We simply use the politics scenario as just one example today of likely trade-offs, but it is equally applicable when the counterplan sets precedent for a state national stage with worse complexity. Do not fall for the illusion of risk created by specific evidence, as readers deciding this would think it out in general principles to see past the logic fallacy that the absence of evidence is not the evidence of absence, it all only seems so one-sided because all states have never acted in unison on one policy before. The counterplan is not like the central advocacy and **should be seen as the authors’ value claims:** we should not add more items to the federal agenda, prioritize the war risk of the politics scenario, and that the federal government should handle some policies via a unified fifty-state platform. **That politics and state delegation has always been a double contradiction.** If we agree on all the claims made, readers would then call on the **party to properly schedule the plan to avoid the political cost by delegating to the unified state platform other relevant policies to horsetrade with governors** per party line and to assure their constituents that **the political scenario is prioritized** due to its nuclear war risk. Readers can agree policymakers can mutually hold both sides’ value claims and resolve on intrinsic issues and still support the plan should be done in principle. Federal action gives up the opportunity for a competing patchwork of state education and abortion policies. There’s **no principled reason** that states should do this plan only, so convincing society would also entail an unforeseeable new political platform of unified state action. National action does not give up the opportunity to transition our government partially to states as national action.

## Distortion of the Literature Base
States counterplans that fiat uniform action distort the literature base. Debate thought leader Galloway (2020) furthers, “the states counterplan thrives by distorting the literature base to answer it \[...\] \[N\]ot even the notion that something is currently considered to be a federal only activity protects the aff. I've seen federal nuclear policy, federal transportation policy, and federal lands policies Lopez'd back to the states.” If every issue can be reassigned at will, the precedent collapses federalism into an incoherent free-for-all. When the counterplan does not reflect a real-world shift to state action, its benefits cannot be compared reliably. Intervening variables dominate speculative snippets. The federalism modeling, which depends on stability, is undermined by **arbitrary reassignment of authority**. When processes can be redefined arbitrarily, a claim like 'at least we educate about the process' justifies any construction at all. Participants cannot be expected to produce evidence answering unrealistic scenarios. Debate distortion creates an impression that all issues would be done better via unified state action. Transitioning makes states equal in the national agenda with the same political cost models. The harm lies in the precedent: **domestic topics get choked off under contrived comparisons**, or outweighed by war risk on the foreign-policy agenda. Our purpose is to parallel real-world discussions to train future leaders. Affirmatives defend **not just plan-focus but that their speech supports their thesis**. Even if educational, it moots in-depth preparation on publicly persuasive topic details and both issues have no solutions. While authors assume the status quo, readers would still reinterpret to support the plan under a hypothetical transition. The government considers hundreds of issues daily on intrinsic merits, any of those can also cause tradeoffs. We have **not prepared research for obscure hypotheticals**, so judges cannot evaluate their impacts as they seem appealing without scrutiny. We will learn the implementation methods when we get to it in an unforeseeable future. But we limit our debates to what articles we can find in real-world considerations in order to persuade the readers on relevant issues intrinsic to that matter. **Readers decide principle values on intrinsic issues, then institutions handle what would happen** as circumstances dictate.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, judges should disregard negative claims that do not establish either **intrinsic harms or a feasible opportunity cost grounded in real-world advocacy**. Debate is not a contest between two affirmatives. The negative must prove that the affirmative inherently sacrifices a better, feasible solution that addresses the concerns. Some may object to a debater writing this. But only debaters apply it this way — no external expert addresses such coordination. The purpose of debate is to educate what is persuasive to an average reader’s comparison, not to abandon real-world constraints and delegate all issues. Debaters may write similar final judgments “slayers” to end repeated generic debates.
