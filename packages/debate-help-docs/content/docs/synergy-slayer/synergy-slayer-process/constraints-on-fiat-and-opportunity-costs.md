## The Standard for Fiat
**Interpretation — the Batterman standard for legitimate fiat.** Negative fiat is bounded by the two constraints Batterman (2021) revives from Solt (1989): a counterplan is fiatable only when it is (1) an action a domestic public could support a public actor could take and (2) a proposal the topic's own policy literature actually advances against the affirmative's harm area. We can only interpret articles in their intended context to an audience comparing the U.S. state action to feasible alternatives discussed. A counterplan that fails either prong is not a competing policy but an argumentative non sequitur — one the decision-maker answers with further action they control, not with rejection of the plan. Under this interpretation the states counterplan is not fiat of a rival policy at all: reassigning federal questions to fifty coordinated states is a proposal the topic literature never advances, so it forfeits the second prong and asks the judge to fiat a uniformity the record cannot support. The judge should weigh only intrinsic, evidenced opportunity costs (Batterman, 2021). We can also add that the affirmative is limited to **advantages that are intrinsic** to the plan and its internal mechanisms. Claims about external benefits invite the response that those marginal impacts are better addressed by other means, disconnected from the core concern at issue. The affirmative defends the general implementation process conceded in cross-examination. Critics may argue **from alternative mindsets**. Extrinsic concerns shift away from engagement and toward contrived comparisons that **no reader or researcher evaluates**.

## Infinite Hypotheticals: When We Relate the Unrelatable, We Justify Injustice
 **Infinite hypotheticals block any action and kill comparative research**. Historical examples show opportunity costs intrinsic to the central concern can persuade decision-makers. Historians suggest students practice debate tactics on how to dissuade Truman’s 1945 nuclear use decision (Burns, n.d.). Advisors seeking to dissuade Truman from using nuclear weapons could have proposed striking oil infrastructure instead — an intrinsic alternative that served his aim of demonstrating will-to-use, since the enemy depended on oil. Arguments to bomb a different city for unrelated reasons struck Truman as a waste of time and made his original choice more certain. The intrinsic alternative engaged the decision; the extrinsic one was set aside. Another example is the 1993 Bosnia crisis, where "the belief that high-flown words matter more than rational calculation" led some to criticize action "without proposing a practical alternative," resulting in thousands dead and delayed intervention "close to the one that realists had initially proposed—and the one that had also been roundly condemned on moral grounds" (Gvosdev, 2005). When a counterplan introduces only marginal external benefits, the presumption should favor the plan. We must presume against the argument’s validity since we did not have time to research **adequately to test its truth** in-round. Such arguments teach something discredited, dressed in jargon that pressures a judge to credit a high-caliber team.

## Alternative Causes Justify Mitigation, Not Rejection
Gostin and Wetter (2022) argue that ethical policymaking requires confronting foreseeable tradeoffs rather than pretending one can act in isolation while ignoring downstream effects. When policies generate tradeoffs, **ethical decision-makers mitigate side effects**. If renewable energy policy harms fossil fuel employment or strains foreign relations, readers presume policymakers would pursue retraining programs or shift trade to renewables. The original policy remains intact while additional measures address secondary harms. Extrinsic objections therefore do not function as decisive opportunity costs. **Alternative causes and tradeoffs** **justify additional action**, not rejection of the plan. No author assumes policies happen instantly, so politics is just an example of one of the possible tradeoffs. Many authors advocate a broader platform agenda, not seeing a single plan in the lens of a vacuum.

The permutation tests any claim for synergies that overcome contradictions of what we advocate readers should support. Some even ask, “Should humans give the planet back to others and to aliens?” Advocates of voluntary extinction do not take their own advice as then the movement dies with no one to advocate against fertility (Manjoo, 2022). Going against the value of protecting life from nuclear war is a logical fallacy of reverse causality that can never be read to persuade anyone to avoid a policy so as to be negligent to public safety or war risk. Authors and the public would still favor the plan for short-term stability to avoid blame for harming nature and justifying global war, which makes their movement fail to persuade. A movement could bioengineer a virus for a controlled extinction while minimizing harm to nature, and would therefore support the plan to avoid times of war which destroy all groups and resources. Judges should infer an author’s intent and not pretend as if weighing which side has more contrived quotes would ever justify amorally jumping to simplified conclusions by conceding that “the plan stops extinction, which can actually be good.” We cannot assume as being true to make conclusive judgments inferring the morality of all extraterrestrial species. Society believes in aliens but we have nothing verifiable as evidence to interpret. Our brains evolved to prioritize survival and have no choice, or else another species would take our place. The plan appeals to common values and public safety risk by showing scenarios that in the worst case escalate to nuclear war. No one would see it as necessary to avoid since there are many intervening actions and too many what-if scenarios of how outcomes can turn the other way. There will always be an unknowable number of unknowns about the universe, but quantum fluctuation risk means default to “try or die” logic. No decisionmaker would speculate as if they can be reliably certain that one nuclear war will remain small and teach us to avoid a bigger war. They will try the same logic fallacies of appeal to unknowable absolutes, like quantum war or time-travel, to justify amoral ends. These authors justify advocating a nuclear strike on an enemy, such as the precedent Hiroshima already set. We can agree in principle to start a war elsewhere, and/or to control extinction with a virus, just to show they are not opportunities the plan gives up.
