1. **Spreading is emergent, not essential:** Fast talking is the side effect of time rules, not part of debate. Word limits make arguments legible and publicly persuasive, and aggregated into issue outlines they could unite news coverage.
2. **Read on screen, speak the summary:** Debate AI's opt-in format pairs by argument style and caps quotes at the speed an audience can silently skim (~300 words a minute), while the speaker reads warranted summaries aloud. The public agrees: the most watched debate video has 400+ such comments.
3. **Quality over quantity:** A 2,000-word evidence limit means faster delivery cannot add evidence, so teams pick their strongest cards and explain why they apply. Counting unread underlined words in DOCX files closes the loophole.
4. **Answering Morris:** Judges cannot discourage speed while demanding answers to every argument. A word cap attacks that incentive directly instead of asking debaters to slow down.

==What does fast talking have to do with debate? It doesn’t: spread is just the emergent effect of time rules that skews how we choose which points seem persuasive==. Only because no one had a better way – until now. ==Word limits make arguments more legible and publicly persuasive, modeling how classrooms and online forums could host substantive disagreement==. Aggregated into Wikipedia-style issue outlines, they could also ==unite international news coverage and help defuse cycles of mutual distrust==. The collective purpose involves ==producing the top hundred articles on divisive issues to unite the public== on topics such as economic regulation and social policy. "If not us, then who? If not now, then when?” ==This call to personal responsibility underscores the importance of the collective consciousness==. Debate AI has an opt-in feature not only to ==pair by argument style excluding critiques and process generics==, but also to ==exchange speeches with word count limits on how fast the audience can silently skim quotes at \~300 w/min. The speaker reads aloud warranted summaries like a research paper while judges & opponents read highlighted quotes on-screen==. On the most watched debate video, there is 400+ such comments (Wired 2013):

> "why not turn it into a debate via writing? It would have the very same benefits"

> "the speaking literally doesn’t matter. the judge and opponent has the text you’re reading, and can read along, the only reason to speak it is that it only gets counted if you say the words out loud."

> "Nobody (outside of high school apparently) ever wins a policy argument by speaking like a hyperventilating auctioneer. To my mind, this competition formalizes every bad habit that young people have in public speaking and turns it up to an eleven."

> "It seems like this style of competitive debate has taken something that could be both informative and entertaining and turned it into a side show to take advantage of an ill-conceived point system. I guess it qualifies as a skill, but it's about as useful as being able to speak Klingon. With either ability, the most they can accomplish is impressing the handful of other people capable of understanding their babble. They certainly aren’t going to win arguments in the real world like that."

> @JeanRobinson28: “This concept of "spreading" (speed reading debate) has ZERO \-- repeat ZERO \-- application outside of academia yet is the standard that Communications Majors are required to aspire to in preparing them for a career in "real life". The Americans with Disabilities Act requires reasonable accommodations for disabled students but there is no way to accommodate this form of debate. It should be ILLEGAL considering that it \*automatically\* excludes students with speech impediments, hearing issues, and some kinds of learning disabilities (like dyslexia) which are all protected by the ADA.”

We should listen to crowd wisdom that the Emperor of speed has no clothes. Lawyers affirm that federal law ADA protects text-to-voice in debate, which forces word credits: “The Title II regulation lists examples of auxiliary aids and services, but is not exhaustive. Specifically listed are qualified interpreters, note-takers, real-time computer-aided transcription services, \[...\] text to voice and audio recordings. \[...\] The obligation applies to all aspects of a student’s educational experience, not solely to academics” (Johns 2023). The U.S. Department of Education (2016) furthers that, “a student who has trouble concentrating, reading, thinking, organizing or prioritizing projects, among other important tasks, because of ADHD may have a disability and be protected under Section 504.” This means that disability protection is not limited to difficulty speaking or hearing; ADHD-like limitations affecting understanding information qualify. Consider how fast-speed and many off-cases are exploited to skew mid-game and to exacerbate negative bias: “An ethnographic analysis is needed to understand complex public debates within their cultural and social context and from the perspective of the participants. \[…\] Manipulative argumentation may involve, for instance, ‘calculated overcommunication’: the strategic production of irrelevant and overly repetitive text and talk with an aim of distracting and exhausting the opponents” (Hansson & Reijven 2025, 193).

As a solution to this, ==a 2,000-word== credits ==limit reduces the incentive to speak at extreme speeds because== we can focus on persuasion using paraphrases at will and still get credit for underlined words in submitted excerpts. NSDA rules already allow paraphrasing, and ==the== online platform ensures the audience has real-time access to the context. We would closer approach the style ==of== academic papers and make persuasive videos on current issues. A natural understanding of debate and persuasion would involve both speech and written documents that audiences can carefully evaluate to trust the ethos appeals. ==Limiting evidence volume should make debate more accessible== by giving participants and judges less material to process and more opportunity to understand each argument. ==A fixed word allowance rewards quality over quantity== by encouraging teams to ==select their strongest evidence instead of overwhelming opponents with repetitive quotations. A smaller evidence record creates more room to compare whether competing sources actually address the specific claim being debated==. The limit encourages teams to ==explain why their evidence applies to the relevant policy, population, and circumstances== rather than relying on loosely related findings. ==Counting unread, underlined words in submitted== outline ==files prevents teams from bypassing the allowance== by shifting additional evidence from oral delivery into written submissions. ==Shared access to a manageable evidence record supports deeper cross-examination of a source’s wording, reasoning, and qualifications==, building on the benefits Morris (2014) identifies for document access. Morris identifies the underlying problem: ==judges struggle to discourage speed while continuing to demand detailed responses to every argument. A word cap is a proposed way to address that incentive directly, rather than simply asking debaters to slow down==. It would reduce the reward for speed, although it would not by itself guarantee slower delivery. ==By constraining evidence volume, the rule aims to shift competitive success from rapid accumulation toward clear explanation, substantive engagement, and persuasive comparison==.
