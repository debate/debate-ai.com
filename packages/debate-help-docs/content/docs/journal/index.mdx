**Synergy Slayer** is the journal's series on the *concerned reader* standard. Its two papers in Volume 1 answer the two most common ways negative strategies avoid the plan: [critiques that reject it](/docs/journal/synergy-slayer-critiques), and [counterplans or tradeoffs that are not intrinsic to it](/docs/journal/synergy-slayer-intrinsicness).

## Issue contents

### Volume 1 (2026)

    Argues that critical theory is misapplied when it is used to preclude debating the plan. Quoted evidence works as a lens, not a gateway; "the system is doomed" is a risk, not a certainty; and a critique cannot demand the affirmative "rethink first" without a feasible alternative. Part I sets the concerned-reader standard, Part II names the logic fallacies (original sin, the Nirvana fallacy, equal epistemic standing), Part III offers the alternative: vote for synergy, test theory in action and build a rainbow coalition, and Part IV answers the major critical theories one by one (rejecting the system, capitalism, security, Heidegger, biopolitics, discursive sound-bite sophistry, Nietzsche, settler colonialism).

    Applies the same standard to counterplans and disadvantages. The negative wins only by proving an intrinsic, evidenced tradeoff that the plan necessarily sacrifices within the same controversy. Remote hypotheticals, speculative scenario chains and arbitrary institutional shifts do not disprove the plan, because policymakers can mitigate secondary harms with sequencing and later action. It shows why the fifty-state counterplan fails the literature test and leads back to federal coordination, and why activist ballots that reject opponents as complicit offer no plan and foreclose persuasion.

## Contributor notes

- **Scope.** Original research on competitive debate (Policy, Lincoln-Douglas, Public Forum, parliamentary and college formats), argumentation theory, judging, debate pedagogy, and AI systems that research, outline or evaluate arguments.
- **Length.** Full papers run 5,000 to 40,000 words; shorter research notes run 2,000 to 5,000 words.
- **Abstract and keywords.** Open with an abstract of up to 250 words and 8 to 25 keywords.
- **Citations.** Cite evidence with author, qualifications, date, title, source and a link or DOI, so every in-text citation can carry a hover card. Follow the [Site format](#site-format) below: Chicago style, with evidence presented as underlined cards. Readers can switch the references page between Chicago, APA, MLA and Harvard.
- **Figures.** Number each figure and give it a caption. Supply images at web resolution with alt text.
- **Originality.** Submissions must be the authors' own work and not under review elsewhere. Disclose any use of AI tools in drafting or research.
- **Archiving and DOI.** Accepted papers are archived on Zenodo with a DOI and published as a web edition on debate-ai.com.

## Site format

Every paper in the journal follows the same citation and style rules. They are Chicago style, with one addition for evidence that the site requires for National Speech & Debate Association (NSDA) compliance.

1. **Base format: Chicago style.** Otherwise, standard Chicago applies. See [Chicago style](/docs/features/cite-format#chicago-style) for the rules that matter most.
2. **Two citation forms are allowed.** Citations may be numbered (the notes-bibliography form, with footnotes or endnotes) or author-year. Choose one and use it throughout a paper. If you number your citations, you must still introduce the author in the text: name them when you first cite them, and do not leave the name only in the note.
3. **Evidence is presented as underlined cards.** To meet the NSDA requirement, each piece of evidence must give:
   - the quoted passage, with the underlined excerpt required: underlining marks the portion of the passage that is actually read and relied on; and
   - a one-sentence summary that gives the context of the evidence and the author's qualifications.
4. **Permitted adaptations.** These are the only departures from standard Chicago:
   - **Silent changes to fit a quotation into a sentence.** You may change capitalization and punctuation without marking the change.
   - **Page numbers for books, in citations only.** List all the relevant page numbers for a book.
   - **Shortened years.** The year may be shortened to its final two digits, with an apostrophe in front, as in '26 for 2026.

An underlined card looks like this (a summary of the context and the author's qualifications, then the citation, then the quoted passage with the relied-on excerpt underlined):

> Peter D. Feaver, a professor of political science at Duke University, argues that debaters cannot set aside the policy consequences of the theories they use.
>
> **Feaver '01** — Peter D. Feaver, Professor of Political Science, Duke University. "Proliferation and Security." In *Twenty-First Century Weapons Proliferation: Are We Ready?*, edited by Peter D. Feaver and James M. Ludes. Frank Cass, 2001, 178.
>
> <u>Ignoring the policy implications of theory is often a sign of intellectual laziness</u> \[…\]

The underlined words are the excerpt the author relies on. Everything else in the quoted passage stays in the card so that readers can check the context.

## Submissions

Format citations and evidence as set out in the [Site format](#site-format). Send the manuscript as a `.docx`, Markdown or Google Docs link to **[support@debate-ai.com](mailto:support@debate-ai.com)** with the subject line "Journal submission", along with each author's name, affiliation and a one-line biography. Every submission gets an editorial read; papers in scope go to review by at least one referee, and authors hear back with a decision and comments.

## Reading tools

Each paper's pages carry the same reading tools: a **Marks** menu that holds every marking tool: switches that show or hide the underlining, the highlighting, the short highlighting and emphasized words separately (highlights take your app theme's colors, in light and dark mode); **Auto underline**, **Auto highlight**, **Auto short highlight** and **Auto emphasize words** modes that mark whatever you select (select marked text to unmark it, no hotkey needed; highlighting is long by default, short highlighting is a smaller secondary stroke for the few words you'd read in a short version, and emphasizing boxes a phrase, bolds it, tints it and underlines it); **AI highlight & underline**; **Reset to default highlighting**; and **Remove all marks**, which clears every highlight and underline of every kind (Reset brings the page's own back). Your own highlights look exactly like the page's. Then there's **Read aloud**, hover cards on every in-text citation, and a references page you can switch between Chicago, APA, MLA and Harvard.

The tools sit in the header bar, right after the search box, each as an icon with a small label; hover one to see what it does. The last one, **Info**, shows the page's stats: words, reading time, sections, figures, citations and highlights.
