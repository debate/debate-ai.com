1. **Complementary perspectives:** Lott, Pinker, Sterling-Folker and Wyn Jones show that **security analysis needs both material threats and their social construction**, so realism and constructivism correct each other, and debate should test both a threat's credibility and the assumptions behind it.
2. **Preventive planning under uncertainty:** Rachman, Munster and Macy show that **rejecting security rhetoric neither stops others from securitizing nor avoids self-fulfilling effects**, and Chernoff calls demands for predictive precision a straw man. Liotta argues that **creeping vulnerabilities require early strategic planning**, so competing forecasts should be compared before the options narrow.
3. **Worst cases fill the void:** Fitzsimmons shows that **rejecting prediction lets decision-makers' worst-case beliefs fill the gap**. Knudsen, Mutimer, Wæver and Kavka show security must be engaged rather than left to the military, and Huysmans notes debaters lack the securitizing power of presidents, so **case debate is where threat perceptions get de-escalated**.
4. **Qualified securitization and humane responses:** Wæver and Elbe show that a **qualified, human-security framing can head off escalation** while keeping health priorities. Lloyd and Kleinman show that **counting the dead, facing fear together and representing suffering restore responsibility** and mobilize responses.
5. **Rational coping and foresight:** Beres and Wu show that **confronting death and reducing avoidable risk is rational coping**, and Bleiker rebuts the compassion-fatigue claim. Kurasawa, Dabelko and Schultz show that **disaster scenarios spur preventive foresight**, so public debate links critical reflection to prevention without treating any prediction as certain.

1. The decision frame for the round’s ==security== calculus ==combines attention to material threats with== counterplans to solve ==how those threats are socially constructed==, drawing on realism and constructivism to correct each other, explains critical theorist Lott (2004): “Critiques ==do not demonstrate an understanding of the role that material threats play== in national security matters or the negative consequences of ignoring those material threats. Their alternative analytic focus often rejects the traditional state 'security dilemma.' \[…\] Such an occurrence does not fulfill the requirements of a security framework that seeks theoretical rigor and policy relevance. It is a necessary (but insufficient) component of a more comprehensive understanding of security. The potentially positive political vision that emerges from political constructivism balances the negative vision provided by realism and suggests an opportunity to overcome culturally constructed threats.” The two can work together, writes Sterling-Folker (2002): “Realism ==and constructivism need one another to correct their own worst excesses==. \[…\] Common ontological ground is possible \[…\] to reread realism as an explanation of the process of global institutional transformation itself, but \[…\] realism must collaborate instead with an approach like constructivism, which is capable of addressing the evolution of particular social content.” Case ==debate examines both the credibility of a particular threat and the assumptions that make it appear threatening==. They should have read counterplans to stop others from seeing us as threats. This distinction matters because recognizing that political actors construct threats does not mean ignoring them is harmless, as one still needs a plan to overcome others securitizing us as threats. This view is grounded in human nature, as influential psychologist Pinker (2002) writes: “violence is universal across human cultures. \[…\] Even among modern nation-states, raw self-interest is an important motive for war. \[…\] But denying the logic of violence makes it easy to forget how readily violence can flare up.” Nobel laureate field-leader Kahneman & Renshon (2009) confirm that “psychology suggests that policymakers come to the debate predisposed to believe their hawkish advisors. \[…\] ==A bias in favor of hawkish beliefs and preferences is **built into the fabric** of the human mind==. \[…\] **In 40 years of psychological research, we were startled by what we found: All the biases in our list favor hawks**. These \[…\] incline national leaders to exaggerate the evil intentions of adversaries.” Recognizing these tendencies does not require accepting that insecurity is inevitable; instead, it makes efforts to prevent conflict important.

2. ==How do critics stop China or fundamentalists from securitizing==? “Believers in the self-fulfilling prophecy have got the argument the wrong way around. \[…\] The ==real sources of the current tensions are Chinese actions==, particularly Chinese threats” (Rachman 1996). ==Rejecting security rhetoric as root cause misapplies projecting mindsets==, when these authors simply guide us for how to work within its flaws, not to wish away the realist mindset as if it is a valid opportunity cost to debating risk in order that worse ideologies do not creep in when accidents occur. Their self-fulfilling impact claims also securitize: “referring ==to the issue of illegal migration in terms of security (even critically\!) re-enforces the social perception of the latter as a security== issue. \[…\] It can be difficult to untie the migrant from its strong negative connotations and, hence, trying to do so would only help sustaining the social image” (Munster 2004). Hearing critiques securitize their impacts causes us nightmares of overconstructing the danger of security rhetoric. The permutation opens calm dialogue to de-securitize nightmares of demonizing rhetoric. The way teams apply it contradicts the critique, as they preclude the very case debate of risk assessment to improve the evaluation of security claims. Debating predictions is still valuable to persuade: “The critics thus commit the straw \[…\] fallacy by requiring a sort of prediction in IR (1) that few, if any, theorists claim to be able to offer, (2) that are not required by policy-makers for theory-based predictions to be valuable” (Chernoff 2009). This combined approach makes public debate and explicit risk assessment essential to preventive planning, as ==uncertainty is a reason to scrutinize assumptions, not to abandon analysis==. Waiting for dangers to become immediate can undermine effective action: “Although it seems attractive to focus on exclusionary concepts that insist on desecuritization, privileged referent objects, and the ‘belief’ that threats and vulnerabilities are little more than social constructions \[…\] all these concepts work in theory but fail in practice. \[…\] The creation of a sense of urgency to act – even on some issues that may not have some impact for years or even decades to come – is perhaps the only appropriate first response. ==The real cost of not investing in the right way and early enough== in the places where trends and effects are accelerating in the wrong direction ==is \[…\] potentially, military engagement==. \[…\] The international community, must increasingly focus on long-term creeping vulnerabilities in order to avoid crisis responses to conditions. \[…\] Rather than focusing on the security issues themselves, we should be focusing on the best multi-dimensional approaches to confronting and solving them. One approach, which might avoid the massive tidal impact of creeping vulnerabilities, is to sharply make a rudder shift from constant crisis intervention toward strategic planning” (Liotta 2005). The point is not that every forecast justifies urgent intervention, but that ==competing forecasts and responses should be compared before deteriorating conditions narrow the available options==.

3. ==Rejecting prediction== means policymakers’ worst ==fears fill-in==: “The ==burden of proof in any debates about planning should belong to the decision-maker who rejects formal analysis== \[…\] Prediction requires decisionmakers to expose, not only to others but to themselves, the beliefs they hold \[…\] While the complexity of the international security environment may make it somewhat resistant to the type of probabilistic thinking associated with risk, a risk-oriented approach seems to be the only viable model for national-security strategic planning. \[…\] Questioning assumptions is critical, but assumptions must be made in the end. \[…\] History suggests that unchecked scepticism regarding the validity of prediction \[…\] empower parochial interests in decision-making \[…\] Where ==analysis is silent or inadequate, the personal beliefs of decision-makers fill the void==. \[…\] ambiguity allows intuition or wishfulness to drive interpretation. \[…\] ==A decision-maker with no faith in prediction is left with little more than a set of worst-case scenarios== and \[…\] existing beliefs” (Fitzsimmons 2006). Psychology studies confirm the self-fulfilling prophecy is backwards: “‘to speak of catastrophe will just make it more likely to happen.’ Actually, the contrary is nearer to the truth. Psychoanalytic theory and personal experience show us that ==it is precisely what we repress that eludes our conscious control== and tends to erupt into behavior” (Macy 1992). “Desecuritizing issues \[…\] invites the reasonable question: opening up for what? Neo-nazis? War? How can the post-structuralist be sure that "liberating minds" and "transcending limits" will necessarily lead to more peaceful conditions” (Wyn Jones 1999, 120). “Threats have to be dealt with both in terms of perceptions and in terms of the phenomena which are perceived to be threatening. \[…\] because situations with a credible claim to urgency will keep coming back and then we need \[…\] procedures” (Knudsen 2001). “I would reject Wæver’s conclusion that we should seek to desecuritise social life, because I fear that ==such a decision would abandon the entire field of security to the military==. \[…\] We aim to securitise important issues – in order to lay claim to those resources and, perhaps as importantly, to question the claims that the military makes” (Mutimer 1999). “Nations ==are literally incapable of refraining from taking steps believed to be necessary for national defense==. \[…\] Any government that abandoned nuclear deterrence would be quickly ousted” (Kavka 1987).

4. The context of a debate is key: we do not construct enemies to appeal to security of the nation, but foresight against scenarios for global extinction to mobilize support. Our debates are distinct from policymakers, thus “not ==everyone speaking or writing security has an equal chance of producing a securitising effect==. It is usually the case that presidents, police officers or generals speaking security \[…\] is more important than the language of shop assistants” (Huysmans 2006). “Securitization regards the nature of the speaker, their authority and legitimacy. \[…\] An American president has more legitimacy and authority to make a securitizing move than, for instance, a mayor or average citizen \[…\] Securitizing moves do not stand alone but must be negotiated with an audience, which either accepts or rejects them” (Fierke 2007, 107). “The argument that securitization is purely a 'speech act' with no direct correspondence to any external reality \[…\] is a radical relativism where no one account of security is to be privileged" (Dannreuther 2007, 43). “What of those problems that *are* a threat to survival? \[…\] Threats are securitized in order to mobilize and legitimate particular responses \[…\] The peace movement of the 1980s identified nuclearism as a threat \[…\] and generated massive public support \[…\] When ==the label ‘security’ is attached to particular issues, it generates validity-claims that are open to redemption or refutation through argumentation==. \[…\] Questions that might arise during the process of redeeming the validity-claims implicit in this scenario would include, Does the third country really pose a threat?” (Wyn Jones 1999, 109–11). “==The responsibility to face up to potential disasters and intervene before they strike becomes compelling. \[…\] Such scenarios can act as catalysts for public debate and socio-political action==, spurring citizens’ involvement in the work of preventive foresight” (Kurasawa 2004). “‘The full worth of a policy is always yet to be seen. Argumentation offers a momentary pause in the flow of events, an opportunity to look down the present road as well as paths untaken. As deliberation raises expectations that are feared or hoped for, public argument is a way to share in the construction of the future.’ \[…\] Politicians are also known to frame certain issues as threats to the nation. \[…\] Protest against the (in)action of powerful actors is often central to the public communication of social movements” (Hansson & Reijven 2025, 188). ==Debating case should be the starting point to deescalate== threats in current policymakers. Debating specific scenarios can therefore expose unsupported assumptions and challenge exaggerated threats instead of leaving those judgments to policymakers’ unexamined intuitions.

5. ==Desecuritization scholars== such as Wæver ==support debating case== specifics ==to avoid worse ongoing securitization== so as to help in “shaping the continent in a way that ==creates the least insecurity and violence== \- even if this occasionally means invoking/producing 'structures' or even using the dubious instrument of securitization. In the case of the current European configuration, the above analysis suggests the use of securitization at the level of European scenarios **with the aim of preempting and avoiding numerous instances of local securitization** that could lead to security dilemmas and escalations” (Wæver 2000). “The language game of security is, in other words, a *jus necessitatis* for threatened elites, and this it must remain. **Such an affirmative reading, not at all aimed at rejecting the concept, may be a more serious challenge to the established discourse than a critical one, for it recognizes that a conservative approach to security is an intrinsic element**” (Wæver 1996). ==Viewing both a security and non-security issue solves the critique and avoids our turns==, as “those presenting HIV/AIDS as a security issue could be sure to insist that it is not exclusively a security issue, but rather ==a security issue in addition to also being a health issue==. \[…\] The security dimension of HIV/AIDS could then complement, rather than supersede, existing frameworks, and it would not undermine alternative rationales for global health initiatives, or unduly prioritize the needs of the security community over those of civil society. Second, the ethical dangers of securitizing AIDS could be further minimized by framing the illness as a security issue, or as an issue with an important security dimension, rather than as a dangerous and overwhelming security threat. This would still add considerable political gravitas to international efforts” (Elbe 2006). The question “is ==not so much whether to securitize== HIV/'AIDS, ==but rather how to securitize it==. \[…\] Biopolitics can be best mediated by securitizing AIDS primarily through a human security framework emphasizing the needs of ordinary people rather than just elites, and by insisting that policy responses not violate human and citizenship rights” (Elbe 2004). Debates securitizing environment solve ==extinction==: “These considerations can greatly enhance ==the== process of formulating effective solutions ==and winning sustained public attention and support for international environmental action==. Policymakers might therefore be best served by framing international environmental priorities in terms ==of a== broad set ==of== interests, including, but not limited to, security concerns. \[…\] Debates concerning environment ==and== security matters will continue ==to== be instructive” (Dabelko 1997). There is consensus on probability: “There is consensus that there is ==a relatively short window to reduce carbon emissions== before \[…\] chances of extinction from climate change are good” (Schultz 2016). Debate ==connects reflection to preventive action by allowing== us ==to contest inflated dangers, assess credible risks, and compare measures that could reduce vulnerability== without treating any single prediction as certain.

6. Minimizing risks ==we can avoid is rational coping that allows us to live without debilitating fear of death==. “Rather than rescue humankind by freeing individuals from fear of death, this perspective recommends educating people to the truth of an incontestable relationship between death and geopolitics. \[…\] Curiously, therefore, before we can implement such education, ==we will need to reduce the perceived threat of nuclear war== and enlarge the belief that the short-term goal of nuclear stability is within our grasp” (Beres 1994). ==Fearing death ensures human== survival—anxiety affirms life and mobilizes support for its preservation: “Fear ==of death, the ultimate source of anxiety, is essential to human survival==. This is true not only for individuals, but also for states. Without such fear, states will exhibit an incapacity to confront nonbeing that can hasten their disappearance. \[…\] For states, just as for individuals, ==confronting death can give the most positive reality to life itself==. \[…\] When a state chooses to block off such an awareness, a choice currently made by the State of Israel, it loses, possibly forever, the altogether critical benefits of ‘anxiety’” (Beres 1996). Macy (2012) argues that confronting nuclear danger can affirm life’s value by deepening our commitment to protecting future generations: “What I can do is to have the courage and stomach to look at what is threatening life, and to honor the ferocity of my desire that life continue.” Even if security lessens life's value, “What can you do to become less afraid of death? \[…\] Don’t avoid the topic \[…\] Avoidance can make those things loom even larger \[…\] Study with funeral directors found that those who had directed more funerals feared death less” (Wu 2020). Kleinman (1996) defends death rhetoric to influence policy: “Our critique of appropriations of suffering that do harm does not mean that no appropriations are valid. To conclude that would be to undermine any attempt to respond to human misery. It would be much more destructive than the problem we have identified; ==it would paralyze social action. We must draw upon the images of human suffering in order to identify human needs and to craft humane responses==.” Bleiker (2007) concludes that compassion fatigue “critiques have, we believe, been dismissed convincingly by scholarly literature. \[…\] The compassion fatigue position cannot be sustained in an unqualified manner, not least because there is widespread evidence that ==the public often reacts generously when charity organizations appeal==.” Szacki (1995) explains keeping people alive should be state policy which lets people decide their life's value. Morality demands doing impact calculus via war deaths: “Not counting a death is thus to signal that such a death does not matter, that the life lost is not a valued or avowed life. Indeed, a failure to count is often viewed as a means to dehumanize the dead” (Lloyd 2026). “Fear appeals change the context by making visible the speaker’s (a) intent to influence the audience to act in order to avoid fearful outcomes and (b) responsibilities to have exercised good prudential judgment about grounds for fear and a proportionate intentional/affective orientation. \[…\] Vote for Johnson to avoid the fearful outcome of nuclear war. \[…\] Further, remaining silent on the issue could open the Johnson campaign to criticism for failing to warn the public. \[…\] The broader discursive context constrains viewers from perfunctorily dismissing the ad as groundless fear-mongering. Doing so would be a fallible sign of not paying attention to the campaigns” (Innocenti 2026). Watching the Daisy ad lets us feel the impacts more (Library of Congress 2016).
