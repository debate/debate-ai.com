1. ==The critique treats settler colonialism as== transhistorical by applying across era to modern context. ==Its own literature warns that this abstraction erases Indigenous peoples== and sustains the domination it condemns, while excluding the case debate where we could engage to find solutions together: “==studies of settler colonialism== and practices of solidarity ==run the risk of reifying== (and possibly replicating) ==settler colonial== as well as other ==modes of domination==. \[…\] Similarly, for Tsalagis (Cherokees), there is a word, digadatsele’i, which means ‘we belong to each other.’ \[…\] Decontextualized conceptions of settler colonial studies, ‘settler’, and solidarity risk further eschewing Indigenous peoples and thereby reifying the stolen land each of the above is founded upon. \[…\] **==Those who critique settler colonialism through transhistorical representations==** ==are then able to feel good== and satisfied about their criticisms, ==despite== their ahistoricism and decontextualization, and thus ==their own role in actually sustaining colonial power== by failing to attend to its conditions and contingency. \[…\] **What good is solidarity if it cannot attend to the literal (and stolen) ground on which people stand and come together upon**? In this paper, ==we have argued for a contextual approach== to the questions of settler colonialism, settlers, and solidarity. It is ultimately about **accountability to each other**, as the Tsalagi word, digadatsele’i suggests. \[…\] Colonialism ==will not be undone by analysis alone==, but through lived and contentious engagement with the literal and stolen ground” (Snelgrove et al. 2014). Leeds (2013) speaks to the power of ᏗᎦᏓᏤᎵᎢ (*dee-gah-dah-tseh-leh-ee)* as the name of the political issues coalition formed by the three bands of Cherokee Nation in 2009. The role of the ballot should be to write those letters on it: literally doing the permutation that we owe it to each other and to this land with its complex history. ==The authors call for engagement with specific harms on== the stolen ==land, which is exactly what case debate does==. We can already recognize and do activist outside, but there is no reason why specifically now to avoid case. Critics may mock this permutation as John Smith & Pocahontas, but it merely proves ==one can support recognizing stolen land and still debate policy issues== that it does not preclude. Settler colonialism should not preclude policy focus: “However, ==settler processes do not constitute these relationships in their entirety==, and more importantly, they do not necessarily determine the range of possibilities available in our futures. It is crucial that we continue to challenge the politically convenient conflation of settler desires and reality and of the political present \[…\] ==The moment that SCT reveals colonization as ongoing is not necessarily the moment we must give up hope of change==. It could, in fact, be the moment that settler colonialism is revealed as one, very limited, way of understanding and organizing our reality” (Macoun & Strakosch 2013).

2. ==Land claims cannot be resolved by retroactively unwinding history. Taken literally, the critique’s logic has no stopping point==. Nearly every territory on earth has changed hands through conquest, and ==reopening those claims would escalate conflicts== from the Middle East to Europe to East Asia. ==It is hypocritical activism== that lets teens feel edgy like a pop celebrity shouting slogans, ==all in a contrived way to compete against a government plan discussion.. Voting for this critique requires giving up your home==, or hypocrisy sparks worse backlash and makes a mockery of the effort. We should not single out Natives as uniquely victimized: Negatives misappropriate decolonization: “Whether postcolonial criticism has been appropriated by those who did not share its initial critical intentions is a moot question, as its methodological denial of structures and its methodological individualism has facilitated such appropriation. Rather than a critique of earlier radicalisms from the inside as initially intended, postcolonialism in its unfolding has turned into a repudiation of the possibility of radical challenges to the existing system of social and political relations. Its preoccupation with local encounters and the politics of identity rules out a thoroughgoing critique of the structures of capitalism, or of other structurally shaped modes of exploitation and oppression” (Dirlik 1998). “The postcolonial argument is mobilised to serve as an alibi for a cultural colonialism. \[…\] Postcolonial criticism has become absorbed into institutions of power, its arguments appropriated by those who may feel marginal in certain ways, but represent new forms of power” (Dirlik 1999). Apologies cannot transfer title after the fact, as once land has passed through centuries of good-faith purchasers and development, doctrines like statutes of limitations, adverse possession, and protection for bona fide buyers deliberately settle those claims so that civilization does not collapse: “Do Anglo-Saxons take England back from the Normans? That would be civilizational suicide. It is easy to call land stolen, but what about the innocent purchasers who acquired in good faith in the interim? Are they thieves? \[…\] Fortunately, Eilish does not actually practice what she preaches, for otherwise anyone would be free to squat in her mansion, by asserting the property belongs to the Tongva” (Epstein & Raskin 2026). Teams who read and judges who vote for this must give up their houses. Just as pop-singer Eilish was mocked for tokenizing “stolen land” to seem edgy, ==a ballot for a demand nobody intends to honor turns== natives into a tokenized virtue signal provoking backlash, and undermines the real accountability to solve issues. ==Judges are pressured to vote for whichever team reads this critique first==, so teams read it to win, not out of solidarity. That is unfair to debaters, and ==it tokenizes== the indigenous by using their suffering to stir outrage and then aiming that outrage to avoid discussing progress.

Critics replicate discursive violence: “Gates argues that postcolonial theory has created its own double bind whereby one can choose to: empower the native discursively... downplaying the epistemic (and literal) violence of colonialism; or play up the absolute nature of colonial domination, by negating the subjectivity and agency of the colonised, thus textually replicating the repressive operations of colonialism. The theoretical implications are that one is left in a constantly ambiguous position as to the impact of colonialism” (Goss 1996). “Clearly there is a danger in normalizing one historical time or one narrative of modernity as being constitutive of colonialism. Clearly there is a risk, at some level, of recentering the very narrative of European modernity that postcolonial studies aim to unsettle. \[…\] Acontextualism can flatten the story of modernities by implicitly denying any change in its relations from one time to another, from one context to another. \[…\] But it does not work as well in contemporary times, in which the lines separating the East from the West, and the North from the South, are increasingly becoming porous” (Shome & Hegde 2002). Environmentalist focus on Indigenous victims risks obscuring the broader harm of nuclear testing and shielding those responsible from scrutiny.
