1. **One flaw does not erase the case:** V**oting against offensive language pushes attitudes elsewhere** rather than changing them, so nitpicking polarizes and hands politics to the far right. **One discursive flaw cannot decide a round**, much as Eisenberg's "minor amendments" principle keeps contracts enforceable, and Kidner and Krishna show that focusing on discourse can drain urgency and hide real violence.
2. **Avoiding words preserves their power:** Kurtz and Oscarson show that **avoiding offensive words keeps their power to injure**, Carlin and Levasseur show that cynical rhetoric ends policy debate, and Boggs wants discourse examined alongside policy. Isolated word critiques therefore teach petty offense-taking, drive researchers away and act as **selectively enforced viewpoint policing**.
3. **Negative burden:** Because critiques cannot be researched in-round, **the negative must prove its indictment applies to this advocacy**. Berkun shows how rhetorical tricks defend the ridiculous, no author says one claim precludes a whole case, discourse authors seek improvement rather than rejection, and **a plan that works under both mindsets reaches more readers**, whereas inflating any phrase into grounds to ignore everything breeds exclusion.

1. ==The debate community eats its own tail when critics falsely polarize==, looking for any excuse to take offense to so they can win ignoring all else, reducing the mindsets of others to a prejudice of inherent harm. Silencing prejudiced language ==in== debate does not necessarily change debaters’ underlying attitudes; it may simply shift their expression to settings outside the round: “The suppression of prejudice prevents its social expression but does not actually reduce prejudice itself as illustrated by the rebound effect” (Lankester & Alexopoulos 2021). Sexism expressed at home or over lunch may therefore escape the scrutiny it would receive in a debate round, where a judge can challenge it through critical, nonpunitive feedback. “Psychological studies reveal that whenever the government attempts to censor speech, the censored speech – for that very reason \- becomes more appealing to many people. Still worse, when pitted against the government, racist speakers may appear as martyrs or even heroes” (Strossen 1990). **Voting against someone does not cause racism to get “rejected”** and only means people self-isolate from changing mindsets, thus ceding the political to far-right rhetoric, while leftist debate makes a mockery of itself as it looks for trivial nitpicks to exclude and spread toxic negativity about some, who then retaliate worse ways. ==Reading the critique to lower credibility of a field already teaches mindsets, without rejecting the team. The critics claim to reject exclusion, yet== they contrive such exaggerated analogies that ==any nitpick becomes a reason to label your fellow activists== with polarizing labels and then those excluded retaliate worse outside of debate. Indeed, ==this shows why debate is a bad forum for activism== forcing dichotomies to avoid the permutation. ==We need to accept that dissent should feel offensive, not silence those we find uncomfortable. The harm is to assume one can simply cherry-pick a problematic discursive mindset and win== just on that. ==That has never been== and never will be ==how real-world debate works==. One generally needs dozens of claims extended to scrutinize assumptions. ==Many policies may have an unethical tinge, and that is weighed== in relation. Their authors only want to be used not in isolation but as part of case debate to distrust authors using the label “terrorist.” The “**minor amendments” clause of contract law** applies that non-central **==discursive mistakes may not affect enforceability==** (Eisenberg 2009).

2. ==If the negative can win just by== advocating ==rephrasing discourse, then debate will become a best-speech contest==, and the negative can win by translating to promote multi-culture. “==The deconstructive bent of discursive approaches limits their capacity to challenge the structure of modern industrialism==. \[…\] The widespread lack of appreciation within academia of the way in which ==postmodern approaches involving deconstruction promote this insidious conceptual assimilation to industrialism== is an index of the urgent need to develop a psychocultural dimension. \[…\] **==Study of discourse enables one to stand aside from issues and avoid any commitment== to a cause or ideal**, simply presenting all sides of a debate and pointing out the discursive strategies involved. As the physical world appears to fade into mere discourse, so it comes to seem less real than the language used to describe it; and **==environmental issues lose the dimensions of urgency==**.” (Kidner 2000, 65–67). "Analyzing the representation of the war itself \[…\] postmodernist analyses wind up, unwittingly, echoing the Pentagon and the White House in their claims that this was a ‘clean’ war. \[…\] Their preoccupation with representation, sign systems, and with the signifier over the signified, leaves one with little sense of the annihilation visited upon the people and land of Iraq. And, as the Vietnam War proved and Schwarzkopf well realized, ==without that physicalistic sense of violence==, **==war can be more effectively sold==** to a jingoistic public. \[…\] Instead of finding ways to live with these differences and to (if fleetingly) coalesce against the New Right, **==this fratricidal critique is politically suicidal**. **It obliterates the space for a political activism==** ==based on provisional and contingent coalitions==. \[…\] They failed to garner sufficient support to influence state policy. The blackmail inherent in the choice \[…\] between total critique and ‘ineffective’ partial critique \[…\] militates against the construction of provisional or **strategic essentialisms**” (Krishna 1993). **==Treating discourse as grounds to reject is suicidal exclusion==**. ==We should strategically use mainstream discourse for coalitional appeal== to persuade the intended readers to prevent war with policies. Reframing a public debate to “one would not look good supporting that mindset” is egocentric **cynicism:** "==rather than discuss the actual costs or benefits== of Dole's tax plan, ==citizens instead focused upon the rhetorical viability== of such a plan. \[…\] There is no point discussing the issue of tax cuts if tax cuts will never take place. Essentially, **when citizens offer cynical statements during public policy discussions, they effectively bring an end to the public policy debate**" (Carlin & Levasseur 2001).

3. Rejecting discourse worsens its violent power: “To think of words as having an ‘open’ future is to recognize that their authority lies less in their historical than in their present uses; it is to acknowledge that people can revise the meaning of words. \[…\] For **when we choose not to use offensive words under any circumstance, we preserve their existing meanings as well as their power to injure**” (Kurtz & Oscarson 2003). Debate is still a game, and ==it skews the game if critiques are twisted to not answer feasible plans==. Boggs (1997) holds that vital issues “cannot be understood outside the larger social and global context,” or else reactionary elites fill-in and risk survival. ==Critical authors would want discourse examined alongside policy analysis==, such as calls to distrust securitized impacts in tabloid news. Teams cannot isolate this argument to win on it alone, as that teaches ignorance to harms while feeling good about yourself seeming more ethical. In-round impacts are false, since we do not actually hold these views personally, and are role-playing to teach us how to analyse current issues. ==It== falsely conflates to project rejecting a flawed mindset as affecting broader change, when it ==forecloses space for how to persuade change==. Word critiques teach ==a bad practice of looking for petty nitpicks== to dramatize taking offense to so as to reject debating those mindsets, ==which only alienates== and fails to persuade those common mindsets. ==A toxic culture of exaggerated taking offense frustrates researchers==, tokenizing nitpicks from ethics scholars who would not want such namecalling to defeat our common purpose. U.S. culture is in the middle of a mental health epidemic greatly worsened by today’s outrage and cancel culture destroying lives and careers and preventing dialogue. Debate community should do better and fight to reverse this and provide healing and constructive research on common issues, not to fuel division for a easy win. Students cajole judges into thinking they are being more ethical in order to tokenize any criticisms they would rather talk about instead. ==Cheapshots inspire similar tactics, until issue researchers depart== for more meaningful communities, leaving the rejectionists with no one to strawperson. These debates teach nothing other than to nitpick to manipulate groups to exclude people with controversial views. **==Treating one offensive phrase as grounds to ignore every other argument functions as viewpoint policing==**, and it is selectively enforced against unpopular views, exactly the speech the First Amendment protects. ==If some cannot handle== free speech, ==then they do not belong in debate==. Expecting to punish people caught in bad opinions normalizes encouraging to find anything to complain about to retaliate to competitors, and getting vilified groups fired until we destroy our society.

Examples include: utterances loaded with presuppositions \[…\] and the exploitation of generic terms.” (Zenker et al. 2019). ==The negative falsely oversells==: they have no quote saying any one claim precludes evaluating the case. ==Their authors are hoping to help shape sectors—yet== they twist them to reject learning to persuade on specifics. ==Discourse authors are hoping to improve the field==, like suggesting better case speech phrasing, ==and would not want to be used to reject listening to mainstream views==.
