1. **Complementary perspectives:** Lott argues that critiques neglecting material threats cannot provide a sufficiently rigorous, policy-relevant security framework, while Sterling-Folker explains that realism and constructivism need each other to correct their excesses and change actions in context; Pinker underscores that violence and self-interest remain consequential features of human conflict. Debate should therefore scrutinize both concrete dangers and their political construction, recognizing that socially constructed threats are not necessarily imaginary and using criticism to improve consequence analysis.
2. **Preventive Planning Under Uncertainty**. Early planning over unchecked intuition: Liotta argues that creeping vulnerabilities demand early investment and multidimensional strategic planning rather than constant crisis intervention, because delayed responses risk prolonged economic and political frustration and potential military engagement. Fitzsimmons explains that rejecting prediction does not eliminate assumptions but allows parochial interests, and existing worst-case beliefs to fill the analytical void, making explicit assumptions and comparative risk assessment essential even when precise probabilities are unavailable.
3. **Public Debate and Preventive Foresight**. Useful prediction, not certainty: Chernoff argues that demanding predictive precision beyond what theorists claim or policymakers require creates a straw-man objection, while Kurasawa explains that potential-disaster scenarios can catalyze public debate, citizen participation, and preventive action. Debate therefore connects critical scrutiny to practical prevention by exposing unsupported assumptions, challenging exaggerated dangers, and comparing credible risks and responses without requiring certainty or treating any forecast as inevitable.

1. Security is best understood through an approach that combines attention to material threats with scrutiny of how those threats are socially constructed, drawing on realism and constructivism to correct each other’s limitations rather than treating either as sufficient on its own. Lott shows these “critiques do not demonstrate an understanding of the role that material threats play in national security matters or the negative consequences of ignoring those material threats. Their alternative analytic focus often rejects the traditional state 'security dilemma' \[...\] Such an occurrence does not fulfill the requirements of a security framework that seeks theoretical rigor and policy relevance. It is a necessary (but insufficient) component of a more comprehensive understanding of security. The potentially positive political vision that emerges from political constructivism balances the negative vision provided by realism and suggests an opportunity to overcome culturally constructed threats.” This distinction matters because recognizing that political actors construct threats does not mean ignoring them is harmless, as one still needs a plan to overcome others securitizing us as threats. Influential scientist Pinker grounds this view in human nature, as “violence is universal across human cultures. \[...\] Even among modern nation-states, raw self-interest is an important motive for war.” Recognizing these tendencies does not require accepting that insecurity is inevitable; instead, it makes institutions and deliberate efforts to prevent conflict important. Sterling-Folker explains how the two perspectives can work together: “Realism and constructivism need one another to correct their own worst excesses \[...\] \[C\]ommon ontological ground is possible \[...\] This enables us to reread realism as an explanation of the process of global institutional transformation itself, but \[...\] realism must collaborate instead with an approach like constructivism, which is capable of addressing the evolution of particular social content.” Together, these arguments support an approach to debate that examines both the credibility of a particular threat and the political assumptions that make it appear threatening by doing impact defense and counterplans to stop adversaries from constructing us as threats. How do critics stop Russia or fundamentalist insurgents from securitizing? Rejecting security rhetoric as root cause misapplies projecting mindsets, when these authors simply guide us for how to work within its flaws, not to wish away the realist mindset as if it is a valid opportunity cost to debating risk in order that worse ideologies do not creep in when accidents occur.

2. The way teams apply it contradicts the critique, as they preclude the very case debate of risk assessment to improve the evaluation of security claims. Debating predictions is still valuable to persuade, and self-fulling prophecies are rare cases: “The critics thus commit the straw man fallacy by requiring a sort of prediction in IR (1) that few, if any, theorists claim to be able to offer, (2) that are not required by policy-makers for theory-based predictions to be valuable.” (Chernoff). This combined approach makes public debate and explicit risk assessment essential to preventive planning, as uncertainty is a reason to scrutinize assumptions, not to abandon analysis. Liotta explains why waiting for dangers to become immediate can undermine effective action: “Although it seems attractive to focus on exclusionary concepts that insist on desecuritization, privileged referent objects, and the ‘belief’ that threats and vulnerabilities are little more than social constructions (Grayson, 2003), all these concepts work in theory but fail in practice \[...\] \[T\]he creation of a sense of urgency to act – even on some issues that may not have some impact for years or even decades to come – is perhaps the only appropriate first response. The real cost of not investing in the right way and early enough in the places where trends and effects are accelerating in the wrong direction is likely to be decades and decades of economic and political frustration – and, potentially, military engagement. \[...\] \[T\]he international community, must increasingly focus on long-term creeping vulnerabilities in order to avoid crisis responses to conditions \[...\] \[R\]ather than focusing on the security issues themselves, we should be focusing on the best multi-dimensional approaches to confronting and solving them. One approach, which might avoid the massive tidal impact of creeping vulnerabilities, is to sharply make a rudder shift from constant crisis intervention toward strategic planning.” The point is not that every forecast justifies urgent intervention, but that competing forecasts and responses should be compared before deteriorating conditions narrow the available options.

3. Fitzsimmons explains why rejecting prediction altogether lets worse fears fill-in speculative policymaking: “\[T\]he burden of proof in any debates about planning should belong to the decision-maker who rejects formal analysis \[...\] \[P\]rediction requires decisionmakers to expose, not only to others but to themselves, the beliefs they hold \[...\] While the complexity of the international security environment may make it somewhat resistant to the type of probabilistic thinking associated with risk, a risk-oriented approach seems to be the only viable model for national-security strategic planning. \[...\] \[A\]ssumptions must be made in the end. \[...\] \[H\]istory suggests that unchecked scepticism regarding the validity of prediction \[...\] empower parochial interests in decision-making \[...\] \[W\]here analysis is silent or inadequate, the personal beliefs of decision-makers fill the void. \[...\] ambiguity allows intuition or wishfulness to drive interpretation. \[...\] A decision-maker with no faith in prediction is left with little more than a set of worst-case scenarios and \[...\] existing beliefs.” Debating specific scenarios can therefore expose unsupported assumptions and challenge exaggerated threats instead of leaving those judgments to policymakers’ unexamined intuitions. Kurasawa shows how debate is preventative foresight that solves worse threats: “the responsibility to face up to potential disasters and intervene before they strike becomes compelling. \[...\] Such scenarios can act as catalysts for public debate and socio-political action, spurring citizens’ involvement in the work of preventive foresight.” Public debate thus connects critical reflection to preventive action by allowing citizens to contest inflated dangers, assess credible risks, and compare measures that could reduce future vulnerability without treating any single prediction as certain.
