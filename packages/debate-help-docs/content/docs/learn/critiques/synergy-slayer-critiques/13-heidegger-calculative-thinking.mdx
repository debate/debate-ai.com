1. Heidegger’s critique of technology cannot be turned into a simple argument that calculative thinking is bad. Buckley makes the point directly: “This ‘gap’ does not mean that calculative thought is somehow ‘bad,’ or that contemplative thinking is ‘better.’ To judge contemplative thought as superior to calculative thought is to think calculatively, and hence cannot be the task of authentic philosophy.”¹ He adds that Heidegger is not claiming “that the nature of modern science as calculative is to be viewed as negative.” Rather, “Heidegger wants to undermine the exclusivity of calculative thinking without denigrating it.” Heidegger’s aim, then, is to keep calculative reason from crowding out every other mode of thought, not to abolish it. A critique that dismisses technological thinking outright repeats the ranking and calculating it claims to reject.  Even if a society wanted to step back from the technological project, it is doubtful that it could. Kateb explains why: “It is scarcely conceivable that Western humanity—and by now most of humanity, because of their pleasures and interests and their own passions and desires and motives—would halt the technological project. Even if, by some change of heart, Western humanity could adopt an altered relation to reality and human beings, how could it be enforced and allowed to yield its effects? The technological project can be stopped only by some global catastrophe that it had helped to cause or was powerless to avoid. \[...\] \[N\]o one would want the technological project halted, if the only way was a global catastrophe.” Rejecting technology offers no workable path forward. Turning inward in search of Being has political consequences. Graham argues that a culture preoccupied with the self leaves the field open to those who exploit it: “emphasising the problem of the ‘ontological self’ \[...\] confines the navel-gazing, ‘narcissistic’ masses to a permanent present which they self-consciously sacrifice for a Utopian future (cf. Adorno 1973: 303; Hitchens 1999; Lasch 1984: 25–59). Meanwhile transnational businesses go about their work, raping the environment. \[...\] The ‘problem’ of subjective death threatens to give way, once again, to unprecedented mass slaughter.” He concludes that “the numbed condition of a narcissistic society, rooted in a permanent ‘now’, a blissful state of Heideggerian Dasein, threatens to wake up to a world in which ‘subjective death’ and ontology are the least of all worries.” Waiting for Being to reveal itself is not neutral. It is a choice that hands the field to the forces Heidegger’s critics most fear.

2. Ecological reality removes the option of standing still. Bowman acknowledges that “land managers can use the enormous complexity of landscape change and the absence of clear goals as an excuse not to act. But this strategy ignores a basic lesson from environmental history that it is impossible to ‘do nothing’ in dynamic systems. \[...\] I suggest that the most basic goal of land management should be to minimize the rate of species extinction.” Levy reaches the same conclusion and spells out what action requires: “We are nevertheless unable simply to leave the environment as it is. There is a real and pressing need for more, and more accurate, technical and scientific information about the non-human world. For we are faced with a situation in which the processes we have already set in train will continue to impact upon that world, and therefore us, for centuries. It is therefore necessary, not only to stop cutting down the rain forests, but to develop real, concrete proposals for action, to reverse, or at least limit, the effects of our previous interventions. \[...\] It is the development of policies to provide such ecologically sustainable alternatives which we require, as well as the development of technical means for replacing our current greenhouse gas-emitting sources of energy. Such policies and proposals for concrete action must be formulated by ecologists, environmentalists, people with expertise concerning the functioning of ecosystems.” Together these points answer the Heideggerian position. Technical and scientific knowledge is not the problem to escape. It is the necessary means of limiting harms we have already set in motion. Heidegger was right to warn that calculative thinking can crowd out other ways of relating to the world, but rejecting technology is neither coherent nor possible. Retreating from technical knowledge only leaves its direction to others and abandons the people and ecosystems that need protection. The responsible path is to use science and technology deliberately and ethically, as tools for repairing the harms we have already set in motion. The permutation centers of case debate to persuade which calculative thought would replicate specific errors, but the critique framework precludes the very discussion space their authors want.
