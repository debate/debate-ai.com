1. **Enabling is not causing:** Dean shows Foucault denies that mass slaughter follows logically from power over life, saying it "presupposes agencies," so the negative must explain why this particular policy would lead there. Zimmer reads Foucault's impact passage as opposition to nuclear sovereign power, which makes debate a way to check it.
2. **Institutions shape biopolitics:** Keulartz, Connolly, Lemke, Dickinson and Englehart show that biopower produces as well as constrains. Democratic rights, pluralism, public debate and rule-bound bureaucracies, not biopolitics itself, decide whether it turns genocidal.
3. **Context and comparison:** Quotes about rationality cannot be applied without looking at what the aff actually defends. Huber shows that using Foucault to win a competitive ballot compromises his ideas and shuts down clash, Katz warns that local emancipation can just redistribute oppression, and Parry shows biopolitics cannot simply be rejected, so judges should weigh concrete benefits against what the alternative can actually do about the harm.
4. **Criticize while working with government:** Foucault's call to work with government "and yet be restive," Derrida's conditional sovereignty, Wendt's bracketing, and Krause and Williams show that analyzing state policy is not endorsing statism. The aff defends its consequences, not every belief of its authors, and critiques should be researched counterplans or indicts, not demands that the aff do the negative's research.
5. **Institutions check atrocity:** O'Kane, Kershaw and Pinker show that institutions, pluralism and international organizations restrain modern atrocities, so reframing away from the concerned reader is like a doctor sued for malpractice answering the grieving family with philosophy. Hitler's refusal to compromise, misreadings of Marx, Mertus and Won show that absolutist root-cause thinking repeats history's errors: "only a Sith deals in absolutes."

1. Even if the negative proves that biopolitical rationality enables mass death, ==enabling an outcome is not sufficient to cause it==. Dean examines Foucault’s impact quotation and concludes that ==mass death does not logically follow from sovereign power over life==, rather ==it is a correlated factor that needs genocidal agencies==: “Consider this striking passage from Foucault […] ==I don't mean that mass slaughters are the effect==, the result, the logical consequence ==of our rationality==, nor do I mean that the state has the obligation of taking care of individuals since it has the right to kill millions of people. Neither do I want to deny that mass slaughters or social care have their economic explanations or their emotional motivations. […] Foucault suggests that the right of mass death does *not* follow from the power over life. In the earlier discussion of bio-politics he argues: 'if genocide is indeed the dream of modern powers, […] its exercise in advanced societies *presupposes agencies* capable of governing, and slaughtering, whole populations” (Dean, 1994). “Foucault shows that ==the genocidal character of National Socialism did not simply arise from its extension of bio-power== […] ==democratic forms of government== is that they ==deploy two instruments to check the unfettered imperatives of bio-power== […] By 'governing through freedom' and in relation to freedom, ==advanced liberal democracies are able to differentiate their bio-politics from that of modern totalitarian states”== (Dean, 2004). Dean considers Foucault’s genocide passage, and ==only field expertise can distinguish true warrants== from ones that just look reasonable to debaters. Even if biopolitical institutions can enable violence against populations, that impact “presupposes agencies” thus ==the negative must explain why this particular state’s policy would have that outcome== in a specific case. Foucault’s passage explains how biopower can justify killing some populations in the name of protecting the life of others—even to the point of genocide. Sovereign power is analytically distinct: it concerns the authority to take life and exercise violence. Zimmer (2025) builds on Foucault to argue that nuclear weapons transform this relationship because their destructive potential threatens not merely segmented populations but all existence. Nuclear extinction therefore cannot be understood simply as another instance of sacrificing some lives to preserve others. Building on this distinction, political authority is needed to prevent nuclear war—but its task must be to constrain and coordinate sovereign power. Zimmer contextualizes the Foucault impact quote as in context of why he opposes sovereign power of nuclear weapons, distinct from biopower. Debate is the very vision of checking sovereign power to stop nuclear use. We can advocate to denuclearize and end the military. Foucault’s impact quote is warning that state policies left uncriticized may escalate to war, but the critic precludes scrutinizing case specifics that persuade on sensible policies.

2. ==Biopolitics can therefore create opportunities for public participation and freedom== as well as constraints; ==its effects depend on institutional arrangements== and the specific programs people pursue. Keulartz (1998) emphasizes its productive dimension: ==“Biopower is not negative and prohibitive but positive and productive==; its aim is not to curtail and restrict life with the help of numerous do's and don'ts but, conversely, ==to protect, support and propagate life==. Under its regime, ==death fell into discredit==: public executions were abolished, the death bed was hidden in the wings and suicide was surrounded by taboos. The cult of noble blood shed in defence of the sovereign was replaced by the cult of healthy sex in the service of life.” This shows how ==Foucault’s reference to “blood shed in defence of the sovereign” was in context of prior eras==, whereas modern times appeals to biopolitics through positive-peace values. “Agamben contends that biopolitics has be­come intensified [...] What a joke. Every way of life in­volves the infusion of norms [...] Every way of life is biocultural and biopolitical.” (Connolly, 2004). “Agamben’s analysis is too state-centred, or rather, it relies on a limited conception of the state which does not take into account important political transformations since the Nazi era \[...\] \[H\]is argumentation remains committed to exactly the juridical perspective that he so vividly criticizes. \[...\] It is more and more the scientific consultants, economic interest groups, and civil societal mediators that define the beginning, the end, and the value of life.” (Lemke, 2005). Dickinson (2004) furthers how debating in the political system checks modern biopolitics from genocide: ==“Nazism was a product not so much of a particular modern set of ideas as of a particular modern political structure==, one that could realize the disastrous potential of those ideas. What was critical was not the expansion of the instruments and disciplines of biopolitics, which occurred everywhere in Europe. Instead, it was the principles […] ==In democratic societies, biopolitics has historically been constrained by a rights-based strategy== […] \[T\]he specific nature of social programs matters […] ==\[T\]here is no necessary correlation between rationalization and authoritarian politics==: the opposite seems in fact to be at least equally true […] ==It is not so easy to impose technocratic ambitions on the public==, particularly in a democratic state […] \[T\]hey ==institutionalized pluralism, solicited participation, enforced public debate==, and effectively sabotaged simple authoritarian government. […] \[O\]rganizations engaged in elaborating biopolitical discourses of various kinds, and working to mobilize the authority and resources of the state to achieve the ends they defined.” Englehart (2003) explains how “non-bureaucratic systems are always dangerous because there is no systematic accountability, no way to check the abuses of the powerful. Because of their size and complexity, rational-legal bureaucracies are much more rule-bound than the alternatives. Even if the rules are unjust, their systematic nature promotes accountability. \[...\] The monopoly of violence and rule of law together create the potential for rights. Indeed, despotic regimes often attack their own bureaucracies, precisely because they represent a point of accountability and transparency, and thus may empower those outside.”

3. The particular context of the round also matters: ==quotations about rationality cannot simply be applied without examining what the affirmative actually defends. We came to a tournament to present one side of an argument==, remain open to relevant fields of thought, and use existing state mechanisms where ==necessary—not to adopt a narrow or immutable worldview. These authors’ arguments support comparing relevant discourses through case debate== rather than rejecting the debate because its discourse is imperfect. Huber (2016) uniquely speaks to how Foucault is misapplied in debate: “Yet ==the negative team always compromises Foucault’s ideas when they attempt to win the ballot. Foucault did not envision his ideas through a competitive lens==, so naturally they do not apply when the negative team is attempting to explain to a judge why they should be validated […] This is less preferable because ==debate would become too exclusive==, as the general populace can understand a state actor taking an action much easier than they can understand major rhetorical change. […] ==Marginalized individuals may prefer modes of proof like evidence and logic== because they are detached from their personal experience because that experience is traumatizing.” Huber argues that using Foucault to demand a competitive ballot compromises his ideas and marginalizes clash, especially for participants who may prefer evidence to identity activism. Katz warns that ==local emancipation can merely redistribute== oppression: “as long as global economic and political structures remain unchanged and unchallenged, ==local emancipations can only be redistributions== — redistributions that actually support existing social relations by merely shifting the greater burdens onto others who are less capable of achieving their own local emancipation.” (Katz, 2000, p. 147). Parry (2005) furthers, ==“biopolitics, while perhaps not necessarily good, is at least not wholly bad==. […] \[T\]he ills laid at the feet of biopolitics are not entirely its fault and that they are in any event the costs of ‘progress.’ […] ==\[T\]here is no reason to believe we are any less happy than people were in the past== […] The comforts and controls of biopolitics will appeal to many people. […] ==Foucault, for example, never charted a way out of biopolitics== beyond developing an individual aesthetic […] The flaw in this diagnosis is that it treats the alienated individual as problematic, as if there were an acontextual, dehistoricized thing known as ‘an individual’ that is capable of being alienated or not […] To the extent the ‘problem’ of the alienated individual has significance in the contemporary world, in other words, it is a direct consequence of the enlightenment and *can only be solved through* biopolitics.” ==Parry challenges the possibility of simply “rejecting” biopolitics==, making comparison essential: ==the judge should weigh the affirmative’s concrete benefits against the alternative’s demonstrated capacity== to address harm rather than reject state action.

4. ==Foucault’s critique== of the history of how state manages life ==should encourage students to question underlying assumptions through debate, not abandon policy analysis==: “The history of ideas \[…\] becomes therefore ==the discipline of interferences==, the description of the concentric circles that surround works, underline them, relate them to one another, and insert them into whatever they are not” (Foucault, 1972, p. 137). ==Students would use the call to check systemic assumptions as a starting point for case research==: develop counterplans with solvency turns, indict expert credibility, offer advantage takeouts, or explicitly oppose government action as a categorical principle even when the harm remains ongoing. Instead, ==critiques too often shift the research burden onto affirmatives==, demanding that they discover whether the negative’s critical scholars have something relevant to say. Foucault (1988) supports doing both—critically questioning government and debating the consequences of working with it: ==“We must escape from the dilemma of being either for or against==. After all, it is possible to face up to a government and remain standing. ==To work with a government implies neither subjection nor total acceptance==. One may work with it and yet be restive. I even believe that the two things go together. D.E. ‘After Michel Foucault the critic, are we now going to see Michel Foucault the reformist?’ […] I think ==the work of deep transformation can only be carried out in a free atmosphere==, one constantly agitated by a permanent criticism.” (p. 154). Derrida (2004) agrees that being “sovereignist only under certain conditions, ==one is already calling into question the principle of sovereignty==. Deconstruction begins there.” ==Teams do not defend the system as good simply because they “use” it==; they seek help through available institutions because those are the mechanisms available to address the harm. ==The affirmative defends its advocacy’s consequences, not every belief held by its authors==, and its participants’ mindsets remain revisable in-round rather than intrinsic commitments. Debates analyzing state policies does not justify ==defending statist assumptions: "we are forced by the nature of the subject matter to bracket the internal processes that constitute the state==, to temporarily reify it, in order to get on with the systemic analysis. \[...\] ==No one, not even the most relentlessly problematizing postmodernist, can problematize everything at once. It all depends on the question one is asking==. Against a book on the states system, therefore, calls to ‘stop reifying the state\!’ should be seen really as calls to ‘change the subject\!” (Wendt, 2000). ==Critiques that treat all state analysis as ideological endorsement similarly essentialize the state== and “change the subject” instead of assessing the advocacy. Krause & Williams (1997) show that “to stand too far outside prevailing discourses is almost certain to result in continued disciplinary exclusion […] ==\[A\]nalyzing state policy need not therefore be tantamount to embracing the statist assumptions== of orthodox conceptions. To exclude a focus on state action from a critical perspective on the grounds that it plays inevitably within the rules of existing conceptions ==simply reverses the error of essentializing the state.” The critics must therefore defend their own essentializing prejudgments== rather than attribute commitments to the affirmative that are not intrinsic to its advocacy. Their concerns can coexist with the policy: ==engaging the state to address a harm is not the same as endorsing the state’s foundations. The== negative is asking to envision doing the case through the process of debate where they would have a strategy based on historical analysis. One common fallacy is advocating doing the case through the lens ==of the critique==. The fiated impacts only become real in decades if we pass the plan in the real world, so by then they can claim credit for helping us find a balanced mindset. Even if they say it is just rejecting the speech, the critique ==becomes a way to have your cake and eat it too==. The cynic is always right. ==You can always do better knowing one more flaw in society==.

5. Political history, institutions, pluralism, and economic freedoms check against modern atrocities. "Modern bureaucracy is not 'intrinsically capable of genocidal action,'" as centralized coercion does not inherently lead to terror; rather, deliberate policy choices drive genocidal actions like secrecy, forced labor, terror systems, or extermination, often diverging from modernity as seen in Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR (O'Kane, 1997, pp. 58–59). "But neither in their acquisition of power nor in their use of it do modern authoritarian rulers much resemble Hitler. International organizations [...] provide barriers" in modern times unlike in prior eras (Kershaw, 2007). “Over the past 60 years the number of wars and number of people killed in wars have plummeted. Worldwide, fewer babies die, more children go to school, more people live in democracies, more can afford simple luxuries, fewer get sick, and more live to old age.” (Pinker, 2014). If war and genocide are inherently risks of modernity, that’s all the more reason to evaluate plans to stop them. We can only see evidence through a concerned reader who can be persuaded on multiple changes. Human nature makes war inherent, but using calls to reject & rethink how modern systems are still imperfect as reasons to avoid detailed research co-opts the very in-round space we have to persuade the public concerned with exigency. We cannot let critics walk away with the wrong mindset of reframing discussions from the intended perspective to justify callousness: one may be like a doctor sued for malpractice telling the family of the deceased to see it as a debate in a medical philosophy journal. "Schoolchildren are being fed the disinformation that people in pre-state societies were inherently peaceable, leaving them uncomprehending, indeed contemptuous, of one of our species' greatest inventions, democratic government and the rule of law" (Pinker, 2002). The critic's systematizing is more likely to replicate history's errors, like Hitler's rejecting compromise with Stalin since mirroring Frederick the Great's absolutist ethic of “reject surrender” came before calculating the lives saved of any compromise (Hirschbiegel, 2004, 2:10). The greater weight of more deaths when the war became unwinnable did not matter, because many would still die as long as the root causes of corruption and diseases existed: the Jews. Marx's red book appealed to talk of the day in 1848 where most blamed the Jewish Rothschild bankers and royal monarchs, but didn't know what caused society’s corruption. Some had a limited grasp of evolution and thought working in city factories would literally evolve one into a machine with no moral soul. Misapplying Marx's red book just led to reductionist systematizing to spill the blood of comrades-at-arms. Even if mindsets cause war, pointing that out is not enough to reverse the cause, so one cannot simply “reject” it; instead, wars precludes mindset movements (Mertus, 1999). Overdetermination shows that no single "root cause" exists, and causal reductionism is the logical fallacy of thinking that isolating a root is somehow a solution to reverse that cause (Won, 2014).Flaws of human nature and future events make all harms inevitable, yet people still find it persuasive that doing one thing is justified despite the broader concern. People do not act on logic flow as an absolute conclusion, but interpret specifics via common values.
