1. Foucault’s critique of the history of how state manages life should encourage students to question underlying assumptions through debate, not abandon policy analysis. Foucault (1972) describes the contextual research debaters already undertake when investigating arguments and applying when relevant: “The history of ideas \[…\] becomes therefore the discipline of interferences, the description of the concentric circles that surround works, underline them, relate them to one another, and insert them into whatever they are not” (p. 137). Students would use the call to check systemic assumptions as a starting point for case research: develop counterplans with solvency turns, indict expert credibility, offer advantage takeouts, or explicitly oppose government action as a categorical principle even when the harm remains ongoing. Instead, critiques too often shift the research burden onto affirmatives, demanding that they discover whether the negative’s critical scholars have something relevant to say. Foucault (1988) supports doing both—critically questioning government and debating the consequences of working with it: “We must escape from the dilemma of being either for or against. After all, it is possible to face up to a government and remain standing. To work with a government implies neither subjection nor total acceptance. One may work with it and yet be restive. I even believe that the two things go together. D.E. ‘After Michel Foucault the critic, are we now going to see Michel Foucault the reformist?’ \[...\] I think the work of deep transformation can only be carried out in a free atmosphere, one constantly agitated by a permanent criticism.” (p. 154). Derrida (2004) agrees that being “sovereignist only under certain conditions, one is already calling into question the principle of sovereignty. Deconstruction begins there.” Teams do not defend the system as good simply because they “use” it; they seek help through available institutions because those are the mechanisms available to address the harm. The affirmative defends its advocacy’s consequences, not every belief held by its authors, and its participants’ mindsets remain revisable in-round rather than intrinsic commitments. The central distinction is between using an institution conditionally and endorsing its foundational assumptions: criticism and policy engagement can coexist.

2. Even if the negative proves that our rationality enables mass death, enabling an outcome is not sufficient to cause it. Dean examines Foucault’s impact quotation and concludes that mass death does not logically follow from sovereign power over life, rather it is a correlated factor that needs genocidal agencies: “Consider this striking passage from Foucault \[...\] I don't mean that mass slaughters are the effect, the result, the logical consequence of our rationality, nor do I mean that the state has the obligation of taking care of individuals since it has the right to kill millions of people. Neither do I want to deny that mass slaughters or social care have their economic explanations or their emotional motivations. \[...\] Foucault suggests that the right of mass death does *not* follow from the power over life. In the earlier discussion of bio-politics he argues: 'if genocide is indeed the dream of modern powers, \[...\] its exercise in advanced societies *presupposes* *agencies* capable of governing, and slaughtering, whole populations” (Dean, 1994). “Foucault shows that the genocidal character of National Socialism did not simply arise from its extension of bio-power \[...\] democratic forms of government is that they deploy two instruments to check the unfettered imperatives of bio-power \[...\] By 'governing through freedom' and in relation to freedom, advanced liberal democracies are able to differentiate their bio-politics from that of modern totalitarian states” (Dean, 2004). “Agamben ignores central aspects of contemporary biopolitics. \[....\] Bare life is no longer simply subject to death; it falls prey to a bioeconomical imperative that aims at the increase of life’s value” (Lemke, 2005). Dean considers Foucault’s genocide passage, and only field expertise can distinguish true warrants from ones that just look reasonable to debaters. Even if biopolitical institutions can enable violence against populations, that impact “presupposes agencies*”* thus the negative must explain why this particular state’s policy would have that outcome in a specific case.

3. Biopolitics can therefore create opportunities for public participation and freedom as well as constraints; its effects depend on institutional arrangements and the specific programs people pursue. Keulartz (1998) emphasizes its productive dimension: “Biopower is not negative and prohibitive but positive and productive; its aim is not to curtail and restrict life with the help of numerous do's and don'ts but, conversely, to protect, support and propagate life. Under its regime, death fell into discredit: public executions were abolished, the death bed was hidden in the wings and suicide was surrounded by taboos. The cult of noble blood shed in defence of the sovereign was replaced by the cult of healthy sex in the service of life.” This shows how Foucault’s reference to “blood shed in defence of the sovereign” was in context of prior eras, whereas modern times appeals to biopolitics through positive-peace values. Dickinson (2004) likewise distinguishes biopolitical capacity from the political structures governing its use: “Nazism was a product not so much of a particular modern set of ideas as of a particular modern political structure, one that could realize the disastrous potential of those ideas. What was critical was not the expansion of the instruments and disciplines of biopolitics, which occurred everywhere in Europe. Instead, it was the principles \[...\] In democratic societies, biopolitics has historically been constrained by a rights-based strategy \[...\] \[T\]he specific nature of social programs matters \[...\] \[T\]here is no necessary correlation between rationalization and authoritarian politics: the opposite seems in fact to be at least equally true \[...\] it seems that biopolitics is almost always acting on (or attempting to act on) people; it is almost never something they do. This kind of model is not very realistic. This is not how societies work \[...\] It is not so easy to impose technocratic ambitions on the public, particularly in a democratic state \[...\] Modern biopolitics did create, in a real sense, not only new constraints but also new degrees of freedom— \[...\] Nevertheless, they institutionalized pluralism, solicited participation, enforced public debate, and effectively sabotaged simple authoritarian government. \[...\] \[O\]rganizations engaged in elaborating biopolitical discourses of various kinds, and working to mobilize the authority and resources of the state to achieve the ends they defined.” Our debate space is the very resistance to the risk of genocide and war.

4. The particular context of the round also matters: quotations about rationality cannot simply be applied without examining what the affirmative actually defends. We came to a tournament to present one side of an argument, remain open to relevant fields of thought, and use existing state mechanisms where necessary—not to adopt a narrow or immutable worldview. These authors’ arguments support comparing relevant discourses through case debate rather than rejecting the debate because its discourse is imperfect. Huber (2016) uniquely speaks to how Foucault is misapplied in debate: “Yet the negative team always compromises Foucault’s ideas when they attempt to win the ballot. Foucault did not envision his ideas through a competitive lens, so naturally they do not apply when the negative team is attempting to explain to a judge why they should be validated \[...\] This is less preferable because debate would become too exclusive, as the general populace can understand a state actor taking an action much easier than they can understand major rhetorical change. \[...\] Marginalized individuals may prefer modes of proof like evidence and logic because they are detached from their personal experience because that experience is traumatizing.” Huber argues that using Foucault to demand a competitive ballot compromises his ideas and marginalizes clash, especially for participants who may prefer evidence to identity activism. Katz warns that local emancipation can merely redistribute oppression when broader political and economic structures remain intact, so the negative shifts burdens onto others: “as long as global economic and political structures remain unchanged and unchallenged, local emancipations can only be redistributions — redistributions that actually support existing social relations by merely shifting the greater burdens onto others who are less capable of achieving their own local emancipation.” (Katz, 2000, p. 147). Parry (2005) explains, “biopolitics, while perhaps not necessarily good, is at least not wholly bad. \[...\] \[T\]he ills laid at the feet of biopolitics are not entirely its fault and that they are in any event the costs of ‘progress.’ \[...\] \[T\]here is no reason to believe we are any less happy than people were in the past \[...\] The comforts and controls of biopolitics will appeal to many people. \[...\] Foucault, for example, never charted a way out of biopolitics beyond developing an individual aesthetic \[...\] The flaw in this diagnosis is that it treats the alienated individual as problematic, as if there were an acontextual, dehistoricized thing known as ‘an individual’ that is capable of being alienated or not \[...\] To the extent the ‘problem’ of the alienated individual has significance in the contemporary world, in other words, it is a direct consequence of the enlightenment and *can only be solved through biopolitics*.” Parry challenges the possibility of simply “rejecting” biopolitics, making comparison essential: the judge should weigh the affirmative’s concrete benefits against the alternative’s demonstrated capacity to address harm rather than reject state action.

5. Wendt (2000) explains why state analysis does not mean defending statist assumptions: "we are forced by the nature of the subject matter to bracket the internal processes that constitute the state, to temporarily reify it, in order to get on with the systemic analysis. So yes, I am saying that we can be constructivists in one respect while reifying in another (Smith); indeed, I am saying we have to be. No one, not even the most relentlessly problematizing postmodernist, can problematize everything at once. It all depends on the question one is asking. Against a book on the states system, therefore, calls to ‘stop reifying the state\!’ should be seen really as calls to ‘change the subject\!” Critiques that treat all state analysis as ideological endorsement similarly essentialize the state and “change the subject” instead of assessing the advocacy. They cannot require the affirmative to defend one consistent mindset, predict the mindsets of every relevant actor, or prejudge those actors on that basis. Although people inevitably engage the discourses of the society they criticize, working within or around those discourses does not establish their validity. Krause & Williams (1997) show that “to stand too far outside prevailing discourses is almost certain to result in continued disciplinary exclusion \[...\] \[A\]nalyzing state policy need not therefore be tantamount to embracing the statist assumptions of orthodox conceptions. To exclude a focus on state action from a critical perspective on the grounds that it plays inevitably within the rules of existing conceptions simply reverses the error of essentializing the state.” The critics must therefore defend their own essentializing prejudgments rather than attribute commitments to the affirmative that are not intrinsic to its advocacy. Their concerns can coexist with the policy: engaging the state to address a harm is not the same as endorsing the state’s foundations. The appropriate test is a contextual comparison of the advocacy’s consequences, the critique’s specific causal claims, and the alternatives—not categorical rejection based on the mere use of state power. The negative is essentially asking to envision doing the case through the process of debate where they would have a specific strategy based on historical analysis.  The biggest cheat-code in debate logic is to do the case better through the lends of the critique.  The fiated impacts only become real in 20 years when we pass the plan, so by then they can claim credit for helping us find a more balanced mindset. Even if they say it’s just rejecting the case, it really becomes a way to have your cake and eat it too. You can always do better knowing one more flaw in society.
