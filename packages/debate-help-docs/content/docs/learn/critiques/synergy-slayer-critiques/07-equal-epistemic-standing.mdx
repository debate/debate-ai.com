1. **Attitudes are not advocacy:** Neither side has a higher claim to truth, and the aff does not defend policymakers' private mindsets. Wendt, Fearon and Wendt, and Ferguson and Mansbach show that action and intersubjective consensus are how thought gets verified, so problematic discourse is only a link of omission, and treating it as preclusive teaches people to reject disaster relief because of who provides it.
2. **No automatic epistemic priority:** A critique must either be a starting point, from which the plan likely still follows, or a credibility indictment; it cannot be a gateway that any case argument could equally claim to be. Foster says epistemic priority must be earned in-round, claiming credit for "perfecting" the plan is politician-style credit-taking, and critics who cannot explain their jargon should be rejected for sophistry.

1. Neither prince nor pauper has higher providence. ==The affirmative does not defend the mindsets of policymakers==, since ==both we and the critic are just as likely to call to change those mindsets==; if policymakers ignore then the solvency deficit applies to both. ==It is a false distinction to say there needs to be more thinking== to understand reality ==before taking action==, as we expand thinking through experimental action, and ==rethinking is how we verify. These framing cards can be reasons to distrust some authors, but one still has to answer the facts of the harms: “What matters more is what there is, not how we can know it==, since we clearly do know things, and the 'how' of this knowledge will necessarily vary with the many different kinds of questions” (Wendt, 2000). “Just as quantum physicists can do their work without any idea how to interpret its ontological implications, ==social scientists too can proceed pragmatically==, remaining agnostic about what society is 'really' made of. […] adopting a stance of ontological pluralism instead.” (Fearon & Wendt, 2002). Ferguson & Mansbach (1991) furthers, “although there may be no such thing as 'absolute truth', ==there is often a sufficient amount of intersubjective consensus to make for a useful conversation.” Otherwise we teach a dangerous habit==: that whenever you hear a religious or military figure announcing disaster relief, you should ==form a prejudice and reject it== until they rethink everything. Instead, ==critical theorists want their readers to reconceptualize the discourse== as needed ==and help. This is a link of omission== due to those ethical mindsets not being in the affirmative's speech. ==Problematic discourse is simply the attitudes== prevalent in pluralist society ==that we engage in order to learn to persuade== those who hold them. If one shifts those attitudes, ==the advocacy can work in either system==. Just as debaters say they do not endorse the gendered language their authors may use, ==we also do not endorse all the thought systems that exist. They claim the affirmative must== "defend their assumptions" ==as an excuse to strawperson us== and to preach to the choir of their favorite critique, contrived as if preclusive.

2. ==A critique does one of two jobs, and it must pick==. Even if they win we must take their critical theory as the starting point, ==the case articles suggest== the judge should presume ==the ending point would still be the plan. Critics cannot be evaluated as a gateway because then any case argument could do that==, like rethink your advocacy though the starting point of “economy does not have war or security impacts.” Even if the affirmative “uses” U.S. society or a capitalist mindset which has other flaws, ==readers would see past imperfect mindsets== to support based on factual prediction. ==Affirmatives don't have to defend that mindset in other instances==, and the only way to convince society to solve other instances is detailed research for all mindsets. ==Their framework is self-defeating because we'd never get to debate a solution== to overcome those other flaws, since they'd just make us defend societal mindsets again instead. ==All they need to win is a risk that their critic could help==, yet it is only defense to say modern systems work well enough. ==Giving us some other school of thought is like an add-on perspective== to appeal to. ==Calling it a wrong starting point is misleading== as ==it== really means to adopt another mindset, which ==does not preclude supporting the advocacy== but simply allows the judge to project it onto society by promoting it in more rounds. ==Reading as credibility reduction is enough to educate==, but claiming credit for making more-perfect an advocacy through the rethinking of this philosophical starting point is teaching one to be a politician who takes credit for another' s proposal as part of one's own broader agenda. ==Epistemic priority must be earned through in-round argument==, not assumed from a critic's posturing as accounting for all similar arguments — =="we must ask on what grounds we can decide that one category of person has superior insight== into reality" (Foster et al., 1996, p. 187). ==A critic is postured to have a deeper claim== that "accounts for" the specific facts of the policy advocate due to a general critic of their flawed system. ==Affirmatives can ask critic teams to define a complex phrase== that they used and ==if they cannot explain then reject them for sophistry. Sophistry leads to media-manipulation== without self-awareness and violates our purpose.
