## 1. Debate pedagogy and learning

### Bill Batterman — *Impact Description vs. Impact Comparison: A Better Way to Teach Impact Debating* (2014)

Batterman distinguishes between **impact description**, in which debaters explain why their own impact is large, likely, or fast, and **impact comparison**, in which they explain why their side’s full risk story should matter more than the opponent’s. He argues that many debaters mistake generic magnitude–probability–timeframe overviews for comparison, even though those overviews often ignore the relative credibility of each side’s uniqueness, link, internal link, solvency, and mitigation arguments. The paper criticizes impact tournaments for abstracting terminal impacts from their causal chains, creating debates such as “nuclear war versus warming” that fail to mirror how judges actually decide rounds. Instead, debaters should compare the probability and quality of each side’s entire argument: for example, a weak link to nuclear war may not outweigh an affirmative with strong solvency, while a weak affirmative link to warming may make a credible negative risk decisive. The key pedagogical recommendation is to teach contextual, case-specific comparison rather than reusable impact-calculus scripts. ### Eric Morris — *Does File Sharing During Debates Enable Effective Judging?* (2014)

Morris examines whether judges should receive speech documents in real time during paperless debate rounds and concludes that debate should move toward broad sharing by default, with an opt-out option for those who object. The paper acknowledges the major concern that judges who can read documents may listen less carefully, become distracted, or privilege text over oral advocacy, but argues that rapid policy debate already creates extreme listening burdens and that speech files can improve comprehension, flowing, citation verification, and accountability. Using listening research and the HURIER model—hearing, understanding, remembering, interpreting, evaluating, and responding—Morris emphasizes that judging is an active cognitive process rather than passive reception. His broader claim is that paperless technology should be treated as infrastructure for more accurate and transparent adjudication, not merely as a convenience for debaters, while judges should remain aware that document access cannot substitute for attention to the actual speech and interaction in the round. ### David Weston — *Crossing the Judge and Student Divide: A Plea for Post-Round Disclosure* (2017)

Weston argues that judges should disclose decisions and explain their reasoning immediately after rounds because disclosure transforms debate from a bare competition into a continuous educational process. His central claim is that the moments after a debate are uniquely valuable: the round is still fresh, students can understand what succeeded or failed, ask questions, and apply feedback in their next round rather than waiting for ballots or the next tournament. He argues that written ballots are useful but insufficient because they may be vague, delayed, inaccessible, or incapable of generating a two-way conversation. Disclosure also improves judging, Weston contends, because judges who expect to explain their decisions are pushed to reflect on their paradigms and articulate more coherent reasons for their ballots. While he recognizes concerns about tournament timing and the drama of delayed results, he concludes that debate should be treated as an extension of the classroom, where feedback and adaptation matter more than suspense around wins, losses, or trophies. ## 2. Topicality, stasis, and debate focus

### Michael Greenstein — *An Activity at Risk: A Call for Topic-Centered Debate* (2014)

Greenstein defends topic-centered policy debate, arguing that the annual resolution should remain the central object of affirmative and negative advocacy rather than being displaced by discussions of identity, personal experience, accusations of oppression, or the rules of the activity itself. He claims that a prearranged topic creates predictable research burdens, recurring clash, innovation after losses, substantive policy learning, and better preparation for real-world advocacy. In contrast, he argues that non-topic-centered affirmatives make preparation difficult and reduce the incentive for deep policy research. The article also contends that evaluating rounds through identity or personal experience can create an uncomfortable and psychologically risky situation in which a ballot seems to endorse or reject an individual’s oppression, background, or social location. Greenstein’s model thus treats topicality as both a fairness norm and an educational prerequisite, though its critique of identity-centered advocacy adopts a controversial and strongly exclusionary account of what policy debate should permit. ### James Crosswhite — *A Fluid Ideal: Dialectical Virtues and the Possibility of Debate* (2019)

Crosswhite frames debate as a fluid ideal that appears across internal reflection, interpersonal disagreement, family and group decision-making, institutions, public deliberation, and digital discourse. He argues that formal procedures—turn-taking, time limits, rules, and voting—matter because they seek to create equal participation and fair decision-making, but procedure by itself cannot make genuine debate possible. Debate also relies on “dialectical virtues”: habits of respecting equal dignity, listening carefully, resisting the exploitation of privilege, seeking a common good rather than tribal victory, remaining open to others, acting with integrity, persevering through impasse, and valuing truth. Crosswhite’s key argument is that rules must remain responsive to social conditions: when one group predictably loses under the same procedures, the legitimacy of debate itself erodes, so equity and adaptation may be necessary to sustain trust. The paper ultimately presents debate as a collective practice that depends on both fair structures and ethical capacities capable of turning disagreement into peaceful, creative, and just change. ## 3. Fiat and counterplan competition

### Mosley-Jensen — *Theory of Intrinsicness* (2009)

Mosley-Jensen defends a limited affirmative right to make intrinsicness arguments against disadvantages, maintaining that a disadvantage should not count if the plan is not a necessary cause of it. The article distinguishes **logical intrinsicness**, where logic alone proves that the plan need not cause a disadvantage, from **empirical intrinsicness**, where evidence claims that an additional action could prevent or mitigate the disadvantage. The author considers logical intrinsicness more defensible because negatives already use comparable counterplan logic to show that affirmative advantages are not uniquely caused by the plan; allowing affirmatives access to similar reasoning therefore creates reciprocity. Empirical intrinsicness is more difficult because it risks unpredictable, unlimited add-ons, but the paper argues that some affirmative flexibility follows from logical policymaking: judges should prefer the best feasible policy package rather than reject a plan merely because another compatible government action could improve outcomes. The limiting principle is that the judge may fiat actions by the resolutional agent, usually the U.S. federal government, but cannot fiat the choices of independent actors such as terrorists. ### Brett Bricker — *Positional Competition: More Than Just a Plan Text* (2013)

Bricker proposes **positional competition** as a middle ground between textual competition and expansive functional competition. Under his model, a counterplan competes not merely because its wording differs from the plan text, nor because the negative invents a “normal means” assumption about how the plan would work, but because the affirmative has explicitly taken a position about implementation in the 1AC, cross-examination, or 2AC and the counterplan differs from that position. For example, if the affirmative claims that only congressional action can generate international modeling, a counterplan using a different actor can be competitive; if the affirmative claims implementation must happen before a particular event, a delay counterplan can compete. Bricker argues this model prevents the affirmative from claiming an implementation assumption for disadvantage purposes while denying it for counterplan competition, but also prevents negatives from using generic delay, consultation, condition, or process counterplans based on unspoken assumptions. His central claim is that affirmatives should be accountable for choices they make, while negatives should not gain counterplan competition from choices the affirmative never defended. ### Michael W. Kearney — *How Durable Is It? A Contextualized Interpretation of Fiat in Policy Debate* (2014)

Kearney criticizes the common assumption that fiat is infinitely durable—what he calls **fiat ad infinitum**—because it treats every hypothetical policy as equally permanent and insulated from political, institutional, cultural, and economic change. He accepts that fiat is needed to focus debate on what should be done rather than whether a proposal can initially pass, but argues that a policy’s post-enactment durability should still be evaluated contextually. His proposed model recognizes institutional variation, because actions by agencies, presidents, Congress, and courts may have different levels of staying power; attitudinal variation, because controversial policies may face backlash or repeal efforts; and temporal variation, because future political realignments, cultural shifts, or economic changes can alter a policy’s prospects. Kearney’s key claim is that debate should not reduce itself to a magical-wand abstraction when topic literature and real policymaking show that policy durability matters. A contextualized fiat model therefore keeps the hypothetical focus of debate while allowing more realistic arguments about whether an advocacy survives the forces that shape actual political decisions. ### Ryan Galloway — *To Consult or Not to Consult: On the Theoretical Legitimacy of the Consultation Counterplan* (2019)

Galloway argues that consultation counterplans should be rejected because their competitiveness rests on the questionable premise that the affirmative must enact its plan immediately and with certainty, while the negative may delay implementation and make it conditional on another actor’s approval. He explains that consultation counterplans usually offer a strategic bind: if the consulted ally agrees, the negative claims to solve the affirmative case and gain a relations net benefit; if the ally disagrees, the negative claims the affirmative’s unilateral action would damage relations. Galloway argues that “should” does not naturally mean “immediately,” and that accepting this interpretation would allow endless generic delay counterplans that evade research on the actual policy controversy. His broader objection is pedagogical: consultation strategies reward negative procedural shortcuts and recycled relations arguments, reducing incentives for topic-specific research about the resolution’s substantive subject matter. The paper therefore supports permitting permutations that delay or condition plan implementation in response to a counterplan and treats consultation CPs as either noncompetitive or theoretically illegitimate. ### Ryan Galloway — *Go Vegetarian: Send the Sacred Cow of the States Counterplan Out to Pasture* (2020)

Galloway argues that the states counterplan should be rejected because it makes domestic policy topics artificially narrow, disincentivizes substantive research, and gives the negative a generic shortcut capable of solving or neutralizing most affirmative cases. His key claim is that the states CP affects the activity before rounds even begin: affirmatives avoid valuable areas of literature because they expect a fifty-state alternative to overwhelm them, negatives can recycle generic federalism arguments rather than investigate the plan’s actual merits, and topic committees may avoid broad domestic topics because the counterplan renders them strategically inhospitable. Galloway concedes that federalism research can be valuable, but argues that a federalism disadvantage can preserve that discussion without allowing a one-size-fits-all alternative to displace nearly all topic-specific affirmative research. His conclusion is that the negative retains ample ground without the states CP, while debate gains more substantive, varied, and educational engagement with domestic policy questions. ## 4. Roles, advocacy, and framework

### Jacob William Justice — *Role Confusion: The Case Against Policy Debate as a Role-Playing Exercise* (2015)

Justice argues that policy debate is commonly but incorrectly described as a role-playing exercise, and that both traditional policy advocates and critics of government-centered debate often rely on this misconception. He distinguishes role-play—taking on an assigned identity one does not ordinarily occupy, such as a prime minister or United Nations delegate—from policy debate, where students are not assigned governmental identities and ordinarily do not pretend to be senators, representatives, or other officials. In policy debate, he argues, students simply defend or oppose the desirability of political actions and may use multiple perspectives within the same round. Justice’s key intervention is that discussing whether the U.S. government should act is not the same as identifying with, endorsing, or becoming the government. He argues that many benefits attributed to role-playing—civic knowledge, perspective-taking, reflection, and engagement with multiple viewpoints—actually derive from switch-side debate and argument about policy rather than simulation. The paper concludes that both defenders and critics of topical policy debate should abandon the role-play metaphor and state their arguments more accurately in terms of the value or harm of governmental-policy discourse itself. ### J. L. Schatz — *Activist Judging: A Question of Objectivity and Pedagogy* (2013)

Schatz argues that activist judging—judges openly incorporating political, ethical, or pedagogical commitments into their paradigms—is potentially more objective and more educational than the ideal of a neutral “blank slate.” He contends that true neutrality is impossible, and that conventional forms of objectivity often hide rather than eliminate the social perspectives that shape judgment, particularly norms associated with white, male, middle-class authority. ### Bob Overing — *Recovering the Role of the Ballot: Evaluative Modesty in Academic Debate* (2015)

Overing critiques the rise of role-of-the-ballot claims as totalizing frameworks that can determine in advance which arguments, impacts, and forms of advocacy count in a debate. He distinguishes a **confident** evaluation model, in which the judge first selects one role of the ballot and then decides the debate exclusively under that framework, from **evaluative modesty**, in which the judge considers both the relative strength of competing frameworks and how well each team performs under its proposed evaluative standard. For Overing, a confident approach can turn a small advantage on framework into the complete erasure of an otherwise overwhelming policy impact; a modest approach avoids this all-or-nothing result by allowing multiple evaluative concerns to retain some relevance. The paper’s main argument is that modesty better fits the pluralism of modern debate, supports meaningful clash between different methods and styles, and prevents the ballot from becoming an overly dogmatic meta-decision. He does not deny that framework matters; he argues that roles of the ballot should be weighed proportionately rather than treated as total exclusion devices. ## 5. Inclusion, resistance, and community

### Jesse Mikolajcik — *Collegiate Forensics and Community Resistance* (2018)

Mikolajcik argues that collegiate forensics remains structured by whiteness even when competitors from marginalized groups achieve prominent success, because the activity’s judging norms, definitions of professionalism, performance standards, institutional rules, and traditions still privilege white-coded forms of speech and authority. The paper examines resistance through critical arguments in policy debate and contrasts that relative flexibility with individual events, where formal requirements and community expectations often limit the ability of competitors to use cultural identity, personal experience, or nonnormative performance as legitimate competitive strategies. Mikolajcik argues that successful competitors of color are often tokenized: their success is treated as exceptional or attributed to identity rather than recognized as evidence of skill and structural transformation.
