1. **Spreading is emergent, not essential:** Fast talking is the side effect of time rules, not part of debate. Word limits make arguments legible and publicly persuasive, and aggregated into issue outlines they could unite news coverage.
2. **Read on screen, speak the summary:** Debate AI's opt-in format pairs by argument style and caps quotes at the speed an audience can silently skim (~300 words a minute), while the speaker reads warranted summaries aloud. The public agrees: the most watched debate video has 400+ such comments.
3. **Quality over quantity:** A 2,000-word evidence limit means faster delivery cannot add evidence, so teams pick their strongest cards and explain why they apply. Counting unread underlined words in DOCX files closes the loophole.
4. **Answering Morris:** Judges cannot discourage speed while demanding answers to every argument. A word cap attacks that incentive directly instead of asking debaters to slow down.

==What does fast talking have to do with debate? It doesn’t: spread is just the emergent effect of time rules that skews how we choose which points seem persuasive.== Only because no one had a better way – until now. ==Word limits make arguments more legible and publicly persuasive, modeling how classrooms and online forums could host substantive disagreement.== Aggregated into Wikipedia-style issue outlines, they could also ==unite international news coverage and help defuse cycles of mutual distrust.== The collective purpose involves ==producing the top hundred articles on divisive issues to unite the public== on topics such as economic regulation and social policy. "If not us, then who? If not now, then when?” ==This call to personal responsibility underscores the importance of the collective consciousness.== Debate AI has an opt-in feature not only to ==pair by argument style excluding critiques and process generics==, but also to ==exchange speeches with word count limits on how fast the audience can silently skim quotes at ~300 w/min.== ==The speaker reads aloud warranted summaries like a research paper while judges & opponents read highlighted quotes on-screen.== On the most watched debate video, there are 400+ such comments (Wired, 2013):

> "why not turn it into a debate via writing? It would have the very same benefits"

> "the speaking literally doesn’t matter. the judge and opponent has the text you’re reading, and can read along, the only reason to speak it is that it only gets counted if you say the words out loud."

> "Nobody (outside of high school apparently) ever wins a policy argument by speaking like a hyperventilating auctioneer. To my mind, this competition formalizes every bad habit that young people have in public speaking and turns it up to an eleven."

> "It seems like this style of competitive debate has taken something that could be both informative and entertaining and turned it into a side show to take advantage of an ill-conceived point system. I guess it qualifies as a skill, but it's about as useful as being able to speak Klingon. With either ability, the most they can accomplish is impressing the handful of other people capable of understanding their babble. They certainly aren’t going to win arguments in the real world like that."

==A 2,000-word evidence limit reduces the incentive to speak at extreme speeds because faster delivery cannot increase the amount of evidence allowed.== ==Limiting evidence volume should make debate more accessible== by giving participants and judges less material to process and more opportunity to understand each argument. ==A fixed word allowance rewards quality over quantity== by encouraging teams to ==select their strongest evidence instead of overwhelming opponents with repetitive quotations.== ==A smaller evidence record creates more room to compare whether competing sources actually address the specific claim being debated.== The limit encourages teams to ==explain why their evidence applies to the relevant policy, population, and circumstances== rather than relying on loosely related findings. ==Counting unread, underlined words in submitted DOCX files prevents teams from bypassing the allowance== by shifting additional evidence from oral delivery into written submissions. ==Shared access to a manageable evidence record supports deeper cross-examination of a source’s wording, reasoning, and qualifications==, building on the benefits Morris (2014) identifies for document access. Morris identifies the underlying problem: ==judges struggle to discourage speed while continuing to demand detailed responses to every argument.== ==A word cap is a proposed way to address that incentive directly, rather than simply asking debaters to slow down.== It would reduce the reward for speed, although it would not by itself guarantee slower delivery. ==By constraining evidence volume, the rule aims to shift competitive success from rapid accumulation toward clear explanation, substantive engagement, and persuasive comparison.==
