# Constraints on Fiat and Opportunity Costs

## The Standard for Fiat

Interpretation — the Batterman standard for legitimate fiat. Negative fiat is bounded by the two constraints Batterman (2021) revives from Solt (1989): a counterplan is fiatable only when it is (1) an action by a public actor that a domestic public could support and (2) a proposal the topic's own policy literature actually advances against the affirmative's harm area. We can only interpret articles in their intended context to an audience comparing the U.S. state action to feasible alternatives discussed. Under this interpretation, State counterplans assume action unsupported by the literature: reassigning federal questions to fifty coordinated states is a proposal the topic literature never advances, so it forfeits the second prong and asks the judge to fiat a uniformity the record cannot support. The judge should weigh only intrinsic, evidenced opportunity costs (Batterman, 2021). We can also add that the affirmative is limited to advantages that are intrinsic to the plan and its internal mechanisms. Claims about external benefits invite the response that those marginal impacts are better addressed by other means, disconnected from the core concern at issue. The affirmative defends the general implementation process conceded in cross-examination. Critics may argue from alternative mindsets. Extrinsic concerns shift toward contrived comparisons that no reader evaluates.

## Infinite Hypotheticals: When We Relate the Unrelatable, We Justify Injustice

*Figure 8 \- History Demands Intrinsic Alternatives*

Infinite hypotheticals block any action and kill research. Historical examples show opportunity costs intrinsic to the central concern can persuade decision-makers. Educational exercises on how to dissuade decision-makers offer useful insights (Burns, n.d.). Suppose advisors seeking to dissuade Truman from using nuclear weapons had proposed striking oil infrastructure instead — an intrinsic alternative that served his aim of demonstrating will-to-use, since the enemy depended on oil. Arguments to bomb a different city for unrelated reasons would strike a decision-maker as a waste of time and make their original choice more certain. The intrinsic alternative engages the decision; the extrinsic one is set aside. Another example is the 1993 Bosnia crisis, where "the belief that high-flown words matter more than rational calculation" led some to criticize action "without proposing a practical alternative," resulting in thousands dead and delayed intervention "close to the one that realists had initially proposed—and the one that had also been roundly condemned on moral grounds" (Gvosdev, 2005). When a counterplan introduces only marginal external benefits, the presumption should favor the plan. We must presume against the argument’s validity since we did not have time to research adequately to test its truth in-round. Such arguments teach something discredited, dressed in jargon that pressures a judge to credit a high-caliber team.

## Alternative Causes Justify Mitigation, Not Rejection

*Figure 9 \- Alternative Causes Justify Mitigation*

Gostin and Wetter (2022) argue that ethical policymaking requires confronting foreseeable tradeoffs rather than pretending one can act in isolation while ignoring downstream effects. When policies generate tradeoffs, ethical decision-makers mitigate side effects. If renewable energy policy harms fossil fuel employment or strains foreign relations, readers presume policymakers would pursue retraining programs or shift trade to renewables. The original policy remains intact while additional measures address secondary harms. Extrinsic objections therefore do not function as decisive opportunity costs. Alternative causes and tradeoffs justify additional action, not rejection of the plan. No author assumes policies happen instantly, so politics is just an example of one of the possible tradeoffs. Many authors advocate a broader platform agenda, not seeing a single plan in the lens of a vacuum. Fettweis (2017) lists five intervening factors that check war: antiwar norms, nuclear deterrence, economic interdependence, democracy, and international institutions. Kaczmarek (2017) explains how the ripple effect of solving the case’s ongoing likely impacts controls and solves extinction escalation scenarios: “intervening in persistent global harms will contribute to existential risk reduction by removing harmful ripple effects in two important respects. [...] Second, by failing to do more now to alleviate global harms our civilization is not just at growing risk of a catastrophe occurring, but also at growing risk of being unable to respond to an eventual existential threat. [...] Therefore, the risk of humanity’s extinction goes down by removing this fodder for catastrophe. [...] So, we are better off even if the threat presents sooner than it would have had global hunger continued to plague the world’s poorest. Therefore, it seems we can expect the threat of catastrophe won’t go up simply by accelerating our development. However, that being said, we are problematically clueless about how particular interventions will shape the far future.” Mosley-Jensen (2009) explains affirmatives should be allowed to make intrinsicness arguments because, under a framework drawing on Solt’s theory, logical arguments show that a disadvantage is not a necessary consequence of the plan (Congress could pass both the plan and health care), while empirical arguments identify additional policies that could prevent it; allowing these arguments reciprocates negative counterplan reasoning and follows logical policymaking, which asks whether the plan should be adopted alongside compatible actions—not whether the plan alone is optimal. Although empirical intrinsicness raises legitimate predictability and excessive-breadth concerns, abusive examples such as a plan to fiat that terrorists not to attack justify limits on permissible agents rather than a blanket prohibition, making logical intrinsicness arguments concerning the resolutional agent especially defensible because they preserve reciprocal reasoning without requiring control over outside actors. In sum, they are simply how the public reader will reason about state actors.

## Extinction and War “Impact Turns” are Not Intrinsic and Teach Negligence

*Figure 10 \- Rejecting Survival Fallacies*

The permutation tests any claim for synergies that overcome contradictions of what we advocate readers should support. Some even ask, “Should humans give the planet back and go extinct?” Advocates of voluntary extinction do not take their own advice as then the movement dies with no one to advocate against fertility (Manjoo, 2022). Going against the value of protecting life from nuclear war is a logical fallacy of reverse causality that can never be read to persuade anyone to avoid a policy so as to be negligent to public safety or war risk. Authors and the public would still favor the plan for short-term stability to avoid blame for harming nature and justifying global war, which makes their movement fail to persuade. A movement could support the plan to avoid times of war which destroy all groups and resources. Judges should infer an author’s intent and not pretend as if weighing which side has more contrived quotes would ever justify amorally jumping to simplified conclusions by conceding that “the plan stops extinction, which can actually be good.” We cannot assume as being true to make conclusive judgments inferring the morality of all extraterrestrial species. Society believes in aliens but we have nothing verifiable as evidence to interpret. Our brains evolved to prioritize survival and have no choice, or else another species would take our place. The plan appeals to common values and public safety risk by showing scenarios that in the worst case escalate to nuclear war. No one would see it as necessary to avoid since there are many intervening actions and too many what-if scenarios of how outcomes can turn the other way. There will always be an unknowable number of unknowns about the universe. No decisionmaker would speculate as if they can be reliably certain that one nuclear war will remain small and teach us to avoid a bigger war. They rely on fallacies of appeal to unknowable absolutes to justify amoral ends. We can agree in principle to address war elsewhere, just to show they are not opportunities the plan gives up.
