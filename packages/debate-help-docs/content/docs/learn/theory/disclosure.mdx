# Disclosure

1. Disclosure in Lincoln-Douglas Debate (LD)
## Overview
Disclosure in LD debate is the practice of sharing the evidence, cases, and arguments a debater uses during a round by uploading them to the circuit wiki after the round ends. This process is intended to promote fairness, transparency, and education within the community by allowing future opponents to see exactly what was read. Disclosure has become a common norm on the national circuit and is generally expected in competitive debate.

Disclosure entails uploading the case you read during each round after the round has concluded. The affirmative will typically disclose the 1AC, and the negative will typically disclose the 1NC. You should not disclose your frontlines or blocks, as that would put you at a strategic disadvantage; you should only disclose what you read. This norm is increasingly being challenged due to the near-universal norm of doing so in policy debate.

Although there is not universal consensus whether disclosure as a community norm is actually good or bad, most debaters have accepted the practice and continue to disclose on the wiki. If you do not disclose, you can expect to have people read [theory](disclosure)(/disclosure-theory) against you. Since many judges themselves are disposed to vote in favor of disclosure theory, many people have concluded that it is more strategic to disclose, even though that means people will have access to your prep after you have read it.

There are some exceptions to the practice worth noting. Some debaters do not disclose positions or performances that are of a personal nature to them. Other debaters do not disclose because their school forbids them to disclose. Also, novices are generally not expected to disclose. These debaters are still at risk of having disclosure theory ran at them; however, they might receive more sympathy from their opponents and judges.

In addition to disclosure after each round, debaters are typically expected to send the 1AC to their opponent 30 minutes before each round to promote more in depth debating. If your 1AC is already open sourced on the wiki ([OpenCaselist](https://opencaselist.com/)), then you can simply indicate that in an email. You are not expected to proactively email your 1AC out, but 99% of the time the neg debater will email you or contact you for it.

## Methods of Disclosure
## Open-Source Disclosure
Debaters upload a word document or file to the wiki that contains their constructive speech with all highlighted tags and evidence cards intact. This document is essentially the same as the speech prepared and shared with opponents and judges in-round. Open-source disclosure is considered the community standard as it provides comprehensive access to the case materials and boosts educational value.

## Full-Text Disclosure
Some debaters choose to paste the full text of their speech document into the wiki cite box. While this method provides access to all content, it lacks evidence highlighting and formatting, making it less ideal. It is rare on the current circuit unless combined with open-source disclosure for clarity.

## Cite Box Disclosure
In this method, debaters disclose shorthand versions of evidence, including tags and the first and last few words of cards, in the wiki cite box. This provides a quick summary of evidence used but may be affected by wiki technical limitations, so debaters often note if their cites are incomplete and advise checking open-source.

## Round Reports
Though less common, some debaters provide round reports summarizing what was read in each speech. These reports help newer debaters understand argument strategies and improve overall community education.

## Correct Tournament Naming
Disclosure entries should use the official tournament names rather than colloquial ones to aid newer debaters in finding events on platforms like Tabroom and to maintain clarity in records.

## Norms and Expectations
- Disclosure is typically done after the round, not before, to avoid strategic disadvantages.
- The affirmative usually discloses their 1AC, while the negative discloses their 1NC.
- Frontlines (strategic arguments) and blocks are generally not disclosed to maintain competitive strategy.
- Novices and certain exceptions (due to personal or school policy reasons) might not disclose but risk facing disclosure theory arguments.
- Non-disclosure can lead to theory challenges and is often viewed unfavorably in judging decisions.

## Benefits of Disclosure
- Fairness: Ensures all debaters have equal access to previously used arguments, preventing unfair surprises.
- Educational Value: Helps debaters learn from each other’s research, improve case construction, and prepare more substantive responses.
- Accountability: Verifies correct citation and ethical use of evidence.
- Community Resource: Builds a shared database of arguments and evidence for ongoing improvement in circuit debate.

## Relationship to Disclosure Theory
Failure to disclose or improper disclosure can lead opponents to run disclosure theory against a debater, arguing that non-disclosure is abusive and grounds for a loss regardless of substantive arguments. Thus, consistent disclosure is also a protective strategy against this theory shell.
