# Cite Format

==How to cite evidence on Debate AI==: the cite on a debate card, the Chicago

## Debate card cite format [#debate-card-cite]

==A debate card has three parts==: the tag, the cite and the card text.

1. **Tag.** One line in your own words that says what the evidence proves,
   written as a claim you want the judge to flow.
2. **Cite.** ==The short cite comes first, in bold: the author's last name and
   a two-digit year==, such as **Feaver 01**. When no date can be found, write
   **ND** for "no date". The full cite follows on the same line: the author's
   full name, ==their qualifications== (why this person is worth quoting),
   the full date, the title, the publication, and the link.
3. **Card text.** The quote itself, copied word for word from the source.
   ==Underline the words you will read and highlight the words that carry the
   warrant==. Mark a cut in the middle with **\[…\]**, and never cut in a way
   that changes what the author meant.

A typical card looks like this:

> **Ignoring policy implications is intellectual laziness**
>
> **Feaver 01** — Peter D. Feaver, Professor of Political Science, Duke
> University, 2001, "Proliferation and Security," in *Twenty-First Century
> Weapons Proliferation: Are We Ready?*, edited by Peter D. Feaver and James
> M. Ludes, Frank Cass, p. 178
>
> <u>Ignoring the policy implications of theory is often a sign of intellectual laziness</u> \[…\]

Read the short cite out loud when you read the card ("Feaver oh-one"), and be
ready to show the full cite and the whole article if the other team asks.

## Chicago style [#chicago-style]

==The research papers use Chicago style== (the notes-bibliography form) for
their bibliographies. The rules that matter most:

- **Alphabetical by last name.** Entries are listed by the first author's last
  name, written *Last, First*. Later authors are written *First Last*.
- **Parts in quotes, wholes in italics.** An article or a chapter goes in
  "quotation marks". A book, a journal, a newspaper or a website goes in
  *italics*.
- **Headline case.** Titles keep their capital letters, as in *Rules for Radicals*.
- **Periods between the parts.** Author. Title. Where it was published. Date.
  Link.
- **The year goes near the end**, after the journal or the publisher.
- **Links come last**, after the date.

The common shapes:

| Source | Chicago bibliography entry |
| --- | --- |
| Journal article | Akbar, Amna. "Non-Reformist Reforms and Struggles over Life, Death, and Democracy." *Yale Law Journal* 132, no. 8 (2023). |
| Book | Alinsky, Saul. *Rules for Radicals: A Pragmatic Primer for Realistic Radicals*. Random House, 1971. |
| Chapter in an edited book | Feaver, Peter D. "Proliferation and Security." In *Twenty-First Century Weapons Proliferation: Are We Ready?*, edited by Peter D. Feaver and James M. Ludes. Frank Cass, 2001. |
| Magazine or web article | Albert, Michael. "Looking Back, Moving Forward." *Z Magazine*, September 1998. znetwork.org/… |

==Inside the paper, a quote is cited by author and year==, either in the
sentence, as in "Fitzsimmons (2006) explains", or after the quote, as in
"(Feaver, 2001, p. 178)". Add a page number when you have one.

On any References page, ==the citation style switcher reformats every entry
into APA, MLA or Harvard== if your class or journal asks for another style.

## Research Paper refDoc standard [#refdoc-standard]

==A refDoc is a paper's References page written as a set of debate cards==, so
every source in the paper can be opened, read and checked in one place. A
paper meets the refDoc standard when its References page has:

1. **A stats table at the top**: highlighted words, underlined words, total
   words and the number of quotes across all the sources.
2. **One numbered entry per source**, in alphabetical order, ==with the Chicago
   bibliography entry as its heading==. The entry starts closed, so the page
   reads as a normal bibliography.
3. **Inside each entry, opened**:
   - a one-sentence summary of what the source is and what the excerpt argues,
   - ==the author's qualifications== in italics,
   - a small count of the underlined and highlighted words,
   - ==the full quote==, underlined and highlighted the same way as a card, with
     **\[…\]** for every cut.
4. **Every quote in the paper has an entry.** If the paper quotes it, a reader
   can find the full card on the References page.

The References count on a paper is shown as a **Refs / RefDoc** badge, next to
its word, page, figure and cite counts.

Use **Expand all** at the top of a References page to open every entry at once.

## Introducing a cite [#introducing-a-cite]

==Introduce a quote with the author's name and a verb== that says what the
author is doing, then give the quote or a close paraphrase. These are common
quote introduction words:

Says, states, writes, notes, explains, argues, reports, suggests, observes, points out, emphasizes, maintains, contends, describes, asserts, comments, remarks, indicates, claims, concludes, highlights, stresses, clarifies, outlines, holds, mentions, adds, acknowledges, warns, cautions, proposes, predicts, summarizes, details, illustrates, underscores, elaborates, examines, interprets, characterizes, questions, challenges, counters, disputes, criticizes, rejects, defends, advocates, insists, concedes, recognizes, admits, reasons, infers, finds, determines, estimates, anticipates, expects, speculates, posits, hypothesizes, conjectures, objects, draws attention to, calls attention to.

Pick the verb that matches the author's stance. ==*Argues*, *contends* and
*insists* signal a position; *warns* and *cautions* signal a risk; *concedes*
and *admits* signal a point against the author's own side==; *says*, *states*
and *writes* are neutral.

The pattern the papers follow:

1. **Author (Year) + verb + the claim in your words**, so the reader knows
   why the quote matters before reading it.
2. **A colon, then the quote** in quotation marks, cut with **\[…\]**.
3. **The page number** after the quote when you have it, or the author and
   year in parentheses if the name was not in the sentence.

## Synergy Slayer examples [#synergy-slayer-examples]

These are taken from *The Synergy Slayer of Critiques in Debate*.

- Fitzsimmons (2006) ==**explains** why rejecting prediction altogether lets
  worse fears fill-in speculative policymaking==: “The burden of proof in any
  debates about planning should belong to the decision-maker who rejects formal
  analysis \[…\]”
- Graham (2000) ==**argues** that a culture preoccupied with the self leaves
  the field open to those who exploit it==: “emphasising the problem of the
  ‘ontological self’ \[…\]”
- He ==**concludes** that== “the numbed condition of a narcissistic society,
  rooted in a permanent ‘now’, a blissful state of Heideggerian Dasein, threatens
  to wake up to a world in which ‘subjective death’ \[…\]”
- Keulartz (1998) ==**emphasizes** its productive dimension==: “Biopower is
  not negative and prohibitive but positive and productive \[…\]”
- Huber (2016) uniquely ==**speaks to** how Foucault is misapplied in
  debate==: “Yet the negative team always compromises Foucault's ideas when
  they attempt to win the ballot.”
- Dean ==**examines** Foucault's quote and **concludes** that mass death does
  not follow from sovereign power over life==.
- Katz ==**warns** that local emancipation can merely redistribute
  oppression==.
- A quote with the cite after it: “Ignoring the policy implications of theory
  is often a sign of intellectual laziness \[…\]” ==(Feaver, 2001, p. 178)==.

The chapter summaries use the same verbs in shorter form: "Feaver **says**
ignoring policy implications is intellectual laziness", "Huysmans **notes**
debaters lack the securitizing power of presidents", and "Liotta **argues**
that creeping vulnerabilities require early strategic planning".

## Good to know [#good-to-know]

- Don't put words in an author's mouth: if the verb overstates the quote (say,
  *proves* for a source that only *suggests*), the other team can call it out.
- A paraphrase still needs the author and year.
- Keep the short cite on a card the same as the in-text cite in a paper
  (author and year), so a reader can match the two.
